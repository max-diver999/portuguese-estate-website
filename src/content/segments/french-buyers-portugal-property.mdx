Quick Answer: a French tax resident who buys in Portugal owns one house that appears in two annual wealth-tax computations, in two countries, on two different valuations. No other buyer in this market has that problem, and the structure French families use by reflex makes the Portuguese half worse.

## One house, two annual charges, two different numbers

Most foreign buyers in Portugal think about annual taxes once: IMI, plus AIMI if the holding is large enough. A French tax resident has to think about them twice, because France assesses the impôt sur la fortune immobilière on worldwide real estate rather than on French property alone.

That produces a situation which is genuinely specific to this cohort. The same Algarve villa is simultaneously an asset in a French annual computation and an asset in a Portuguese one. Neither country is doing anything unusual; they simply both reach the property, and they reach it in different ways.

The differences are what make the planning non-obvious:

- **Different valuations.** IFI works from market value. AIMI works from the VPT, the taxable value the Portuguese tax authority assigns, which commonly sits well below the purchase price.
- **Different thresholds.** The French charge begins at a French threshold on a worldwide base; the Portuguese one begins above €600,000 of taxable value per owner.
- **Different scope.** IFI counts the French house too, so the Portuguese purchase can push a French position over a line that had nothing to do with Portugal.
- **Different levers.** The French position responds to debt and to structure; the Portuguese one responds mainly to how many owners are on the deed.

## Where the SCI reflex costs money

French families hold property through a société civile immobilière as a matter of habit, for succession reasons that are real and well understood at home. Applied to a Portuguese purchase without checking, it collides with the AIMI rules in a way that is easy to state.

| Owner on the Portuguese deed | AIMI allowance | Rate applied |
|---|---|---|
| One individual | €600,000 of taxable value | 0.7% above the allowance, rising in bands |
| A couple holding jointly | €1.2 million combined | 0.7% above the combined allowance |
| A company, including an SCI | None | 0.4% from the first euro of taxable value |
| A company in a blacklisted jurisdiction | None | A punitive rate applies |
| Individual, property below the allowance | Full allowance covers it | No AIMI at all |
| Company, property below any threshold | No allowance exists | AIMI still applies |

Read the last two rows together, because that is the whole trap. A €520,000 apartment with a VPT of €362,000 held by a French couple attracts no AIMI whatsoever. The same apartment held by their SCI attracts AIMI on the entire taxable value, every year, forever.

Whether that cost is worth paying depends on what the SCI is doing for the family in France. Sometimes it genuinely is: succession planning, governance among several children, and the flexibility of holding parts rather than a whole are not trivial benefits. But the decision should be a priced trade rather than a default, and the Portuguese side of the price is set out in the [AIMI guide](/guides/aimi-wealth-tax-portugal-property/) and the [company ownership guide](/guides/buy-property-portugal-through-company/).

## The lever that costs nothing, taken at the wrong moment

The most valuable decision available to a French couple buying in Portugal is also the cheapest, and it has to be made before the deed.

Because the AIMI allowance is granted per owner, a couple who take title jointly hold 2 allowances. That moves the point at which the Portuguese charge starts from €600,000 of taxable value to €1.2 million. For most French purchases in the Algarve and greater Lisbon, that single choice is the difference between an annual charge and none at all.

The reason to settle it before the escritura rather than afterwards is procedural. Adding a spouse to a Portuguese title later is a transfer, and transfers have their own tax and cost consequences. What is free on the day of the deed becomes a transaction 3 years later.

Buyer scenario. A French couple buy a house near Loulé for €900,000, with a VPT assessed at €640,000. Held in one name, €40,000 of taxable value sits above the allowance and attracts the charge. Held jointly, the whole VPT falls under the combined €1.2 million and nothing is due. The paperwork is identical, the price is identical, and the difference persists for as long as they own it.

## What the French side actually responds to

The Portuguese lever is ownership. The French lever is different, and confusing the two produces bad advice in both directions.

1. **IFI is a net base.** Debt properly secured against the property reduces the French base, subject to the French rules on deductibility, which is why financing decisions and wealth-tax position are connected in France and essentially unconnected in Portugal.
2. **The French threshold is reached on a worldwide total.** A modest Portuguese purchase can carry a French household over a line that its French property alone did not reach, which makes the marginal effect of the Portuguese house larger than its own value suggests.
3. **Portuguese debt does not help AIMI.** AIMI is charged on taxable value without regard to borrowing, so the French logic of leveraging into a wealth-tax base does nothing on the Portuguese side.
4. **Residency changes everything at once.** A French resident who becomes Portuguese tax resident leaves the IFI worldwide base for French property alone, which is a far larger change than any structuring, and it is covered in the [183-day residency guide](/guides/portugal-tax-residency-183-days/).

## Why the valuation gap is the part that misleads

The two taxes disagree about what the house is worth, and the disagreement is systematic rather than random. That makes rules of thumb unusually dangerous for this cohort.

VPT is an administrative value built from area, location, age and use coefficients. It is not a market appraisal and it is not intended to be one. For recently built or recently revalued property it can sit close to the price; for older stock that has not been revalued in years it can sit far below. A buyer who assumes the two track each other reasons wrongly in both directions:

- **Assuming VPT is near the price** produces an AIMI fear that does not materialise, and sometimes an unnecessary structure adopted to avoid a tax that was never going to apply.
- **Assuming VPT stays low forever** ignores that a municipal revaluation can lift it sharply, pulling a holding over the allowance in a year when nothing about the property changed.
- **Assuming IFI can be read from the VPT** understates the French base, because IFI works from market value and pays no attention to Portuguese administrative figures at all.

The instruction that follows is short: get the caderneta predial before modelling anything. It states the VPT, it costs nothing, and it converts this entire section from speculation into arithmetic. The [IMI guide](/guides/imi-property-tax-portugal/) explains how that value is built and when it moves.

## What changes if the family actually relocates

Everything on this page assumes continued French tax residency. A French household that moves to Portugal changes its position more profoundly than any structuring could.

| Position | While French resident | After becoming Portuguese resident |
|---|---|---|
| IFI base | Worldwide real estate | French real estate only |
| Portuguese house in IFI | Included | Excluded |
| AIMI | Applies on Portuguese holdings | Unchanged; it is territorial |
| French income tax on Portuguese rent | Treaty allocation applies | Portuguese resident rules apply |
| SCI logic | Still governs French succession | Portuguese consequences now permanent |
| Practical complexity | Two systems, annually | One primary system |

The row that matters most is the second. For a French couple whose Portuguese property is a significant part of their wealth, relocation removes it from the French wealth-tax base entirely, which is a larger effect than any ownership structure available to them. That is not a reason to move, and moving has consequences of its own across income, succession and healthcare. But it does mean the wealth-tax question and the residency question are the same question, and answering the first without the second is how this cohort ends up with an expensive structure solving a problem that a change of address would have removed.

## Pros, cons and how this cohort should sequence a purchase

The advantages of Portugal for French buyers are unchanged by any of the above and remain strong: full EU freedom of movement with no time limit, no fiscal representative requirement, a flight of about 3 hours, and a coastline that competes directly with the French Atlantic at a lower entry level. Administratively this is the easiest foreign buyer cohort in the market, which is exactly why the tax detail gets skipped.

The disadvantages are the ones on this page. Two annual charges rather than one. A structuring habit that is right at home and expensive here. And a valuation mismatch that makes rules of thumb unreliable in both directions.

### Three French households, three different answers

Buyer scenario, run three ways on the same €700,000 Algarve purchase, because the right structure genuinely differs by family rather than by tax rate.

1. **A couple in their 50s, both working, 2 adult children, French wealth already above the IFI threshold.** The Portuguese house adds to a base that is already taxed, so the marginal French cost is immediate. Joint personal ownership keeps the Portuguese side at zero, and the succession question can wait 20 years. The SCI adds an annual Portuguese cost for a benefit they do not need yet.
2. **A couple in their 70s, 4 children, complicated blended family.** Here the SCI is doing real work: it governs who holds what, avoids an indivision among 4 heirs, and settles a question that will otherwise arrive at the worst possible time. The AIMI cost of roughly 0.4% of the VPT each year is a price for that certainty, and across 15 years it is a number worth calculating rather than waving away.
3. **A single buyer planning to relocate within 24 months.** Neither structure is urgent, because relocation removes the property from the IFI worldwide base entirely. The right move is to hold personally, complete the residency step, and revisit succession afterwards from a Portuguese position.

The pattern across the 3 is that the SCI is a succession instrument being asked to do a tax job. Where succession is the live problem, it earns its cost. Where it is 20 years away, the annual charge starts immediately and the benefit does not.

**Sequence, and the first two items are the ones that expire:**

1. Decide the ownership split before the CPCV, not before the deed, because the promissory contract names the buyer.
2. If an SCI is proposed, price the AIMI consequence explicitly before agreeing to it.
3. Obtain the VPT from the caderneta predial rather than estimating it from the price; the gap is often 20% or more, and on stock unrevalued for 10 years it is larger.
4. Model the French position with the Portuguese asset included, since its marginal effect can exceed its own value.
5. Check whether letting is intended, because the [rental income rules](/guides/non-resident-rental-income-tax-portugal/) run on a separate track from everything here.
6. Read the succession position before assuming the SCI solves it, alongside the [inheritance guide](/guides/portugal-property-inheritance-tax-foreigners/).

One last practical note on timing. The Portuguese assessment cycle and the French one do not align, so the first year of ownership produces paperwork from both countries at different moments and neither reminds you about the other. Owners who diarise both dates in the first 12 months stop being surprised; owners who wait for letters discover the Portuguese one arrives in a language and on a portal they have not yet registered for.

Insider tip: ask your French notaire specifically whether their SCI advice accounts for AIMI. Most will say it does not, because AIMI is not a French tax and there is no reason it would be on their checklist. That answer is not a failure on their part; it is the exact seam between two competent advisers where this cohort loses money.
