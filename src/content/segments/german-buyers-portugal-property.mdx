Portugal charges close family nothing, Germany charges the worldwide estate, and because Portugal charges nothing there is no foreign tax to credit against the German bill.

## The asymmetry, stated plainly

Two countries reach a German family's Algarve house at death, and they reach it in opposite ways.

Portugal abolished inheritance tax and replaced it with stamp duty on gratuitous transfers. That charge exempts transfers to a spouse, to descendants and to ascendants. For the ordinary German case — a house passing to a widow, or to children after 40 years of ownership — the Portuguese state takes nothing.

Germany taxes inheritance and gift on a worldwide basis where the deceased or the heir is resident there. The Portuguese house is simply an asset in that estate, valued and charged like any other, with the personal allowances and rate bands, renewable every 10 years, that apply to the relationship between the parties.

Put together, the sentence that matters is short: the country where the asset sits does not tax it, and the country where the family lives taxes all of it.

## Why "no inheritance tax in Portugal" is the most expensive sentence in this market

German buyers hear that Portugal has no inheritance tax and reasonably infer an advantage. The inference fails at a specific point, and the point is worth isolating.

Germany relieves double taxation on inheritance by allowing a credit for foreign inheritance tax **actually paid**. A credit requires a payment. Where Portugal charges nothing, there is nothing paid, so there is nothing to credit, and the German charge stands at its full amount.

| Situation | Portuguese charge | German charge | Net effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| House to spouse or children, family in Germany | None | Full, on worldwide estate | Taxed once, entirely in Germany |
| House to a sibling or friend | Stamp duty applies | Full, with a credit for it | Relief exists, because tax was paid |
| Family already Portuguese resident | None | Depends on the residence position | The German claim may not arise |
| House inside a company | None on the house itself | Shares still in the German estate | Structure does not remove the charge |
| German national recently emigrated | None | Can persist for a period after departure | Timing rather than avoidance |
| Portuguese-resident heir, German-resident deceased | None | German claim follows the deceased | Residence of both parties matters |

The second row is the one that surprises people. A German owner leaving the house to a sibling pays Portuguese stamp duty and receives a German credit for it. A German owner leaving it to a child pays nothing in Portugal and receives no credit. The closer the relationship, the less relief exists, which is the exact opposite of the intuition.

## The treaty that does not exist

Germany has concluded inheritance and gift tax treaties with only a handful of states, and none has been extended to Portugal in the 20 years and more since Portugal replaced its own inheritance tax with the stamp duty regime. Portugal is not one of them.

This is worth stating explicitly because of a specific and common error. Germany and Portugal do have a double taxation convention, and it is a real instrument that governs income and gains. Advisers who are not succession specialists sometimes reason from its existence to the conclusion that the inheritance position is likewise covered. It is not. The 2 instruments address different taxes, and the absence of the second is the whole of the problem on this page.

That absence has 3 consequences for a German buyer:

- **No allocation rule.** Nothing decides which country has the primary claim, because only one country is making one.
- **No tie-breaker on residence.** Where residence is ambiguous, there is no treaty mechanism to resolve it for inheritance purposes.
- **Unilateral relief only.** Germany's own domestic credit rule is the only relief available, and it is conditional on foreign tax having been paid.

## What actually changes the outcome

Given the above, the levers that work are narrow, and most of the things buyers reach for are not among them.

1. **Residence, over time.** The German claim follows German residence and, for a period after leaving, German nationality. A family that genuinely relocates changes the position, and the [183-day residency guide](/guides/portugal-tax-residency-183-days/) covers where the Portuguese side of that line sits. The German side has its own timetable and needs German advice.
2. **Lifetime giving, within the German rules.** German allowances renew on a defined cycle, so transfers made across 2 or 3 decades reach a different result from a single transfer at death. This is German planning applied to a Portuguese asset, not Portuguese planning.
3. **Matrimonial regime and who is on the deed.** What each spouse owns determines what each spouse's estate contains. Portugal recognises the regime, and the [prenuptial and matrimonial regime guide](/guides/prenuptial-agreement-portugal-property/) sets out how that interacts with a Portuguese title.
4. **Which heirs, not which structure.** Because the Portuguese exemption depends on the relationship, the identity of the beneficiaries changes the Portuguese answer even though it does not change the German one.

What does not work is the structure most often proposed. Holding the house in a company does not remove it from a German-resident family's estate, because the shares are in the estate instead. It does, however, remove the individual AIMI allowance of €600,000 and substitute a charge of 0.4% on taxable value from the first euro, as set out in the [AIMI guide](/guides/aimi-wealth-tax-portugal-property/). That is a real annual cost paid for a succession benefit that does not arrive.

## The three questions a German buyer should settle first

None of these is about the property, and all three change what should be bought and in whose name.

| Question | Why it comes before the offer | Who answers it |
|---|---|---|
| Are both spouses German resident today? | It decides whether the German claim arises at all | A German adviser |
| How long would a claim persist after a move? | Emigration does not end it on day 1 | A German adviser |
| Who are the beneficiaries, exactly? | The Portuguese exemption turns on relationship | The family |
| Whose name goes on the deed? | It determines what each estate contains | Both, before the CPCV |
| Does a German will cover foreign land? | Executors need an instrument that reaches it | A German notary |
| Is a Portuguese will also wanted? | It can simplify the Portuguese formalities | A Portuguese lawyer |

The middle 2 rows are the ones that get skipped, and they are the cheapest to fix. Ownership on the deed is free to decide before signing and costs a transfer to change afterwards. The identity of beneficiaries is not a legal decision at all, but it determines whether the Portuguese exemption applies, and a family that intends to leave the house to a niece rather than a child is in an entirely different Portuguese position from the one they assumed.

## Lifetime transfers, and why the timetable is German

Because the German charge is the only charge, the planning that works is German planning applied to a Portuguese asset rather than Portuguese planning applied to a German family.

German personal allowances against inheritance and gift tax renew on a defined cycle, which makes a programme of lifetime transfers, renewed every 10 years, structurally different from a single transfer at death. A family that begins 20 years before the event reaches a materially different position from one that does nothing, and the Portuguese asset can participate in that programme like any other.

Three practical points about doing it with Portuguese land specifically:

- **Each transfer is a Portuguese deed.** A gift of a share requires Portuguese formalities, a notary and registration, so the transaction cost is real and recurs with each tranche.
- **Portugal charges close family nothing on the gift either.** The stamp duty exemption covers gratuitous transfers to spouses, descendants and ascendants during life as well as at death, so the Portuguese side of a lifetime programme is not an obstacle.
- **Fragmented ownership has consequences.** Several owners on one title means several signatures on any future sale, and an indivision among 3 or 4 heirs is a well-known way to make a house unsaleable for years.

That last point is where a structure earns its place, if one is used at all: not to reduce a tax that cannot be reduced, but to make a jointly held asset governable when the family holding it grows.

## Where this leaves the purchase decision

Buyer scenario. A Munich couple, both German resident, buy a house near Lagoa for €650,000 in their joint names, intending to use it for 20 years, roughly 240 months, and leave it to 2 children.

- **During ownership**, the Portuguese position is ordinary: IMI annually, AIMI only if the VPT exceeds their combined allowance, and rental tax if they let it.
- **At the first death**, Portugal charges the surviving spouse nothing. Germany assesses the German estate including the Portuguese house.
- **At the second death**, Portugal charges the 2 children nothing. Germany assesses again, with the children's own allowances.
- **Across the whole 20 years**, no Portuguese inheritance charge ever arises, and no German credit is ever available.

Nothing in that sequence is a reason not to buy. Germany taxes German estates whether the asset is in Bavaria or the Algarve, and the Portuguese purchase has not created a liability that would otherwise be absent. What it has done is remove a relief that German buyers assume exists, and the practical instruction is to have the German succession conversation before the Portuguese purchase rather than after it.

## What the Portuguese side still asks of the heirs

Portugal charging nothing is not the same as Portugal asking nothing. The transfer still has to be processed, and heirs abroad routinely underestimate the administrative side because they have been told there is no tax.

The Portuguese sequence after a death runs roughly as follows, and each step takes time rather than money:

1. **Establish the heirs formally.** A habilitação de herdeiros identifies who inherits under the applicable succession law, and it is the document everything else depends on.
2. **Declare the transfer to the tax authority**, within the Portuguese deadline, even where the exemption means nothing is payable. The exemption is claimed, not assumed.
3. **Register the change at the land registry**, without which the heirs cannot sell, mortgage or in practice do anything with the property.
4. **Deal with the ongoing charges in the meantime.** IMI does not pause for 1 month while an estate is open, and somebody has to pay it and the condominium quotas from the date of death.
5. **Obtain Portuguese tax numbers for every heir** who does not already have one, since the registry and the tax authority both need them.

Each of those is straightforward with a Portuguese lawyer and slow without one. Heirs living in Germany, dealing with a Portuguese process in a language they do not read, commonly take a year or more, and sometimes 24 months, to complete a transfer that carries no tax at all. That delay has a cost of its own: an unregistered property cannot be sold, and a family that needed to sell discovers the constraint at the worst moment.

The practical instruction is to leave the executors a file rather than a puzzle: the deed, the caderneta predial, the condominium contact, the lawyer's details and the tax numbers, kept together and refreshed every 3 years.

## Pros, cons and a checklist that is mostly German

The advantages are substantial and administratively real. Germans are EU citizens, so there is no time limit on occupation, no fiscal representative requirement, and no third-country mortgage treatment. The Algarve and the Silver Coast have deep German-speaking service infrastructure, and the [Silver Coast guide](/guides/silver-coast-portugal-property-guide/) covers a region where that cohort is particularly established.

The disadvantages are concentrated at one moment rather than spread across the years, which is why they are easy to postpone and expensive to postpone.

**Checklist, and note that 4 of the 6 items are German rather than Portuguese:**

1. Confirm the current German residence and nationality position of both spouses, and the period for which a German claim would persist after any move.
2. Establish who the intended beneficiaries are, because the Portuguese exemption turns on the relationship.
3. Decide the ownership split at the deed, since it determines what each estate contains.
4. Check whether a German will covers foreign immovable property and whether a Portuguese will is also wanted.
5. Price any proposed structure against the AIMI consequence before accepting it.
6. Read the [Portuguese inheritance mechanics](/guides/portugal-property-inheritance-tax-foreigners/) so that the executors know what Portugal will and will not ask for.

Red flag: an adviser who says "Portugal has no inheritance tax, so your children are fine". That sentence is true in its first half and wrong in its conclusion, and the gap between them is the entire German charge. French buyers meet the mirror-image problem annually rather than at death — set out on the [French buyers page](/segments/french-buyers-portugal-property/) — and the two should be planned separately.
