  - question: "Which Portuguese taxes can I credit against my US bill?"

Americans frequently arrive expecting to defer a US gain into a Lisbon or Algarve purchase and discover the plan does not exist.   - question: "Is the US-Portugal position worse than for other foreign buyers?"
    answer: "It is different rather than uniformly worse, and it is more persistent. ## The treaty is not the protection Americans expect

Most foreign buyers in Portugal work through a simple sequence: establish where you are tax resident, apply the treaty between that country and Portugal, and the treaty allocates who taxes what. Move, and your old country's claim recedes.

For a US citizen the sequence has an extra step that undoes much of it. US income tax treaties include a saving clause, under which the United States reserves the right to tax its citizens and residents as though the treaty had not entered into force, subject to a narrow list of exceptions. The treaty still does useful work — it defines residence, it settles source, it provides a mechanism for relief — but it does not remove the United States from the picture for someone holding a US passport.

That single provision reorganises the whole analysis. An American who moves to Lisbon has not exited a tax system; they have entered a second one. Every subsequent question is about how the two interact, and the interaction runs through the foreign tax credit rather than through an exemption.

## What is creditable, and what simply costs money

The credit mechanism works on income taxes. Portugal levies several charges on property owners, and they do not all qualify.

| Portuguese charge | Character | Creditable against US income tax? |
|---|---|---|
| Tax on rental income | Income tax | Yes, in the ordinary way |
| Tax on a capital gain at sale | Income tax | Yes, subject to the usual limitations |
| IMT on acquisition | Transfer tax | No |
| IMI, the annual municipal tax | Property tax | No |
| AIMI above the individual allowance | Property tax | No |
| Stamp duty on the purchase | Transaction tax | No |

Read the right-hand column as a cost schedule rather than as tax trivia. For most buyers, IMT is a one-off friction that everybody pays; for an American it is a one-off friction that will never be recovered through any home-country mechanism. The same is true of IMI every year for as long as the property is held.

This is why an American's purchase model should separate the two groups explicitly. The [non-resident IMT guide](/guides/imt-tax-non-resident-portugal-2026/) sets out the acquisition charge and the residency refund route; the point specific to this page is that whatever survives that route is a real cost rather than a credit waiting to happen.

## The reporting layer, which is not about Portugal at all

Americans consistently underestimate this and consistently over-worry about the wrong part of it. The house itself is not the problem.

- **Directly held real estate abroad is not a specified foreign financial asset**, so buying a Portuguese apartment does not by itself create a Form 8938 filing.
- **The bank account does create one.** FBAR reporting is triggered once foreign financial accounts exceed USD 10,000 in aggregate at any point in the year, and a euro account funded for a purchase crosses that on day 1.
- **Holding through an entity flips it.** An interest in a foreign company or partnership is reportable in ways the underlying house is not, so a structure adopted for Portuguese reasons can create US filings that direct ownership would not.
- **The filings are annual and permanent**, not a one-off at purchase, which is the part that shapes the long-run cost of ownership.

None of that is difficult once known. All of it is expensive when discovered in year 3 with 2 unfiled years behind it.

## The exchange that does not exist

A recurring plan among American buyers is to sell a US rental property and roll the proceeds into a Portuguese one under a section 1031 like-kind exchange. It does not work. Section 1031(h) states that real property in the United States and real property outside the United States are not like kind, which closes the route completely rather than merely complicating it.

The consequence is a sequencing problem rather than a tax problem. The US gain is recognised on the American sale, the US tax falls due on the American timetable, and the Portuguese purchase is funded from the net. A buyer who has modelled the purchase price against the gross proceeds is short by the amount of the tax, and discovers it between the CPCV and the deed, which is the worst moment in the Portuguese process to be short of funds.

## How the credit is actually claimed, and where it leaks

The foreign tax credit is not a refund and it is not automatic. It is a limitation calculation, and the limitation is what determines whether a dollar of Portuguese tax actually removes a dollar of US tax.

Three features of that calculation matter to a property owner:

1. **The credit is limited by category.** Rental income and gains fall into 2 separate baskets over the same 12 months, and Portuguese tax paid on one cannot freely offset US tax on another. A year with Portuguese rental tax and no US rental income in the same basket does not produce the relief the headline suggests.
2. **Timing has to line up.** Portugal assesses rental income and gains on its own calendar, with Modelo 3 filed between 1 April and 30 June and payment due by 31 August, while the US year closes on 31 December. Credits claimed in the wrong year are the commonest source of a mismatch that takes 2 filings and often 12 months to unwind.
3. **Excess credits carry, but not forever.** Where Portuguese tax exceeds the US tax on the same income, the excess does not vanish, but using it requires future income of the same character, which a single-property owner may never have.

The practical consequence is that "creditable" and "credited" are different words. An American with 1 Portuguese apartment, no other foreign income and a modest US bill can pay Portuguese tax that produces very little US relief in the year it is paid. That is not a failure of planning; it is the shape of the mechanism, and it should be in the model rather than discovered in it.

## The purchase year, and the four that follow

Setting the sequence out year by year makes the American position concrete in a way a rate table does not.

| Year | Portuguese event | US consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Purchase year | IMT and stamp duty paid at deed | No credit; a permanent cost in the model |
| Purchase year | Euro account funded | FBAR obligation begins, from USD 10,000 aggregate |
| Years 1 to 4 | Rental income taxed in Portugal | Creditable, subject to the basket limitation |
| Years 1 to 4 | IMI, and AIMI above the allowance | No credit; recurring cost |
| Year of a move | Portuguese residency may begin mid-year | US filing continues unchanged |
| Sale year | Portuguese tax on the gain | Creditable, but computed on a different basis |

The last row is where the two systems diverge most sharply and where preparers most often disagree. Portugal computes a gain with its own acquisition-value rules and an inflation coefficient available after 24 months of ownership, applied from year 2, set out in the [mais-valias guide](/guides/mais-valias-portugal-capital-gains/). The United States computes its own basis under its own rules. The two gains will not be the same number, so the credit will not be a clean offset even where the tax is fully creditable in principle.

For an owner letting the property, the Portuguese side of the annual position is covered in the [non-resident rental income guide](/guides/non-resident-rental-income-tax-portugal/); what this page adds is that each of those Portuguese payments then has to find a home on a US return that was going to be filed regardless.

## Where the saving clause changes the actual decision

Buyer scenario. An American couple sell a rental in Denver and buy a €600,000 apartment in Lisbon, intending to move within 24 months and let the flat for those 2 years first.

- **Their Portuguese rental tax is creditable**, so the letting years are broadly a timing and rate question between 2 systems.
- **Their IMT is not.** At the non-resident flat rate that is a 5-figure sum that no US mechanism returns, and it belongs in the entry cost rather than in the tax model.
- **Their eventual Portuguese capital gains tax is creditable**, but the US will compute its own gain on its own basis rules, and the 2 numbers will not match.
- **Their US filing continues throughout**, in every year, whether or not they have moved and whether or not Portugal taxes them.

Compare that with a buyer scenario for a British or German purchaser, whose home obligation adjusts when residence moves. The American's does not, which is why the useful planning question here is not "should we become Portuguese tax resident" — covered in the [183-day residency guide](/guides/portugal-tax-residency-183-days/) — but "what does our combined position look like in each year of the hold".

## The two layers most Americans forget entirely

Federal tax is the layer everybody plans for. Two others reach a Portuguese purchase and are routinely missed until they bite.

**State tax does not follow the federal logic.** US states set their own residency rules, and leaving the country is not the same as leaving a state. Some states release a departing resident readily; others apply domicile tests that survive a move abroad, looking at where a home is kept, where a licence is held, where family remains. An American who moves to Cascais and keeps a house, a driving licence and a voter registration in a state with an aggressive domicile test can find the state's claim outlives the federal planning entirely. There is no treaty between Portugal and a US state, so nothing arbitrates that claim.

**Federal estate tax reaches worldwide assets.** A US citizen's estate is computed on worldwide property, so a Portuguese apartment enters the American estate calculation wherever it sits. Portugal's own position is close to the opposite: transfers to a spouse, descendants or ascendants are exempt from the Portuguese charge, with the stamp duty regime applying to transfers outside that close family. The result is a mirror image of the German case set out on the [German buyers page](/segments/german-buyers-portugal-property/): there, the home country taxes and Portugal does not; here, the home country taxes on a much larger exemption, and Portugal still does not. Both need planning, and they need different planning.

The practical steps are the same 3 in both layers:

1. **Establish the state position in writing before the move**, not afterwards, since evidence assembled in advance is worth more than evidence reconstructed.
2. **Check how the Portuguese asset sits in the estate plan**, including whether a will exists that covers it and which law governs succession to it.
3. **Keep the 2 systems' documents together**, because the executor who eventually needs them will be dealing with 2 jurisdictions and 1 deadline.

None of this makes Portugal a poor destination for an American. It makes the American file thicker than anyone else's in this market, and the cost of that thickness is entirely front-loaded: expensive to assemble once, cheap to maintain across 20 years, and very expensive to reconstruct after the fact.

## Pros, cons and what to do about it

The advantages of the American position are real and rarely stated. Compliance is predictable, because it does not change when you move. There is no departure tax to plan around. And the foreign tax credit, where it applies, works reasonably well on the income side.

The disadvantages are equally concrete. Portuguese property and transaction taxes are a permanent cost rather than a creditable one. The reporting load is annual and continues for as long as the accounts exist. The structures that other nationalities use for Portuguese reasons can generate US filings. And a US preparer who has not handled a Portuguese file will need the Portuguese side explained to them, which is a cost in fees and in time.

**Checklist before the CPCV, in this order:**

1. Model the combined position across the intended hold, not the Portuguese position alone, and treat IMT, IMI and stamp duty as unrecoverable in that model.
2. Confirm the FBAR position for every account that will exist, including any opened solely for the purchase.
3. If a structure is proposed, ask what US filings it creates before asking what Portuguese tax it saves. The [company ownership guide](/guides/buy-property-portugal-through-company/) covers the Portuguese side of that trade.
4. If a US sale is funding the purchase, settle the US tax on that sale before fixing the Portuguese budget, since 1031 is unavailable.
5. Sequence the currency conversion deliberately rather than at the deed date, using the [currency transfer guide](/guides/currency-transfer-portugal-property/).
6. Engage a US preparer with cross-border experience before the offer, not at the following April.

Red flag: any adviser who says the treaty means you will not be taxed twice, without mentioning the saving clause. That sentence is the single most common piece of bad advice given to Americans buying in Europe, and the whole of this page exists because it is wrong in a way that only becomes visible after the money has moved.
