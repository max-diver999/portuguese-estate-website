## Is the trigger the value of the house, or the use of it?

Canadian owners consistently expect a threshold. They ask what the property has to be worth before Ottawa wants to know about it, and the answer they get — a cost amount above CAD 100,000 — sounds like the whole rule. It is only half.

Specified foreign property triggers a T1135 filing above that cost amount, but the definition itself carves out property held primarily for personal use and enjoyment. A holiday home used by the family is exactly that, so a €600,000 villa in the Algarve kept for family summers can sit outside the reporting regime entirely, while a €200,000 apartment in Porto let year-round sits inside it.

That inverts the usual intuition in a way worth stating twice: the expensive house may be exempt and the cheap one reportable, because the test is what the property is for.

## Where the line moves, and what moves it

| How the property is used | Personal-use exclusion | Practical position |
|---|---|---|
| Family holiday home, never let | Applies | Outside the T1135 regime |
| Family home, let for a few weeks around own use | Fact-specific | The point to take advice, not a rule of thumb |
| Let through the season, owner visits briefly | Does not apply on these facts | Reportable, annually |
| Let year-round on a long tenancy | Does not apply | Reportable, and income taxable in both systems |
| Vacant, held for future personal use | Applies on the facts | Intention matters and should be documented |
| Held through a company | Different analysis entirely | Corporate rules replace the personal-use test |

The second row is where most Canadian owners actually sit, and it is the row without a clean answer. That is not evasion on the part of the rules; it is a genuinely factual test, and the useful response is to document the pattern of use from the first year rather than reconstruct it in year 4 when a filing question arises.

## What crossing the line actually costs

Becoming reportable is an administrative event rather than a tax event, and the two are often conflated. Letting the property does not create a Canadian tax on the house. It creates 3 obligations that did not exist before.

1. **An annual T1135 filing**, with its own deadline and its own penalty regime for non-filing, which is separate from any tax owed on the income.
2. **Canadian taxation of the rental income**, on the worldwide basis that applies to Canadian residents, with a foreign tax credit for the Portuguese tax paid under the [non-resident rental income rules](/guides/non-resident-rental-income-tax-portugal/).
3. **A record-keeping standard that has to be met retrospectively.** The cost amount, the income and the foreign tax all have to be stated, in Canadian dollars, for a property whose paperwork arrives in euros and Portuguese.

None of that makes letting a bad idea. It makes the decision to let a decision with a compliance cost attached, and that cost should be weighed before the first booking rather than discovered at the following April.

## The principal residence question people miss

Canada's principal residence exemption is not restricted to Canadian property. A Portuguese house can, in principle, be designated for years in which it qualifies.

The catch is that the designation is exclusive: for any given year, a family unit can designate only one property. Using Portuguese years therefore spends Canadian years, and the trade only becomes calculable much later, when both properties' gains are known.

Two practical consequences follow at purchase, years before the decision has to be made:

- **Keep the records that preserve the choice.** Purchase price, costs of acquisition, capital improvements and dates, kept in a form that survives a decade, are what make the later designation possible at all.
- **Do not let an adviser close the option early.** The choice is made on the eventual sale, not at purchase, and there is no advantage in deciding it now.

Buyer scenario. A Toronto couple buy in Cascais for €540,000 and keep it for family use, visiting 3 times a year. They file no T1135 because the personal-use exclusion applies. In year 6 they begin letting it for 5 months of the season. From that year the property is reportable, the Portuguese rental tax enters their Canadian return as a credit, and the personal-use exclusion no longer covers them. Nothing about the house changed. Their filing obligations changed completely, and the trigger was a decision they made about the calendar.

## Departure is a separate event, and the order matters

The other genuinely Canadian question has nothing to do with reporting. When a person ceases to be a Canadian resident, Canada applies a deemed disposition: most property is treated as sold at fair market value on the day residence ends, and any gain is taxed then.

Foreign real property is generally within that deemed disposition. So a Canadian who buys in Portugal in one year and emigrates 4 years later can face a Canadian tax event on the Portuguese house at the moment of departure, on a gain they have not realised in cash.

That produces a sequencing question with a real answer:

- **Buying long before an intended move** builds a gain inside Canadian residence, which the departure rules then crystallise.
- **Buying after ceasing residence** puts the acquisition outside the Canadian net from the start.
- **Buying shortly before a move** minimises the accrued gain, which is the practical compromise most families reach.
- **Deciding by accident** is the common case, and it is the one that produces an unexpected assessment in the year of the move.

Anyone whose plan includes eventual relocation should settle this before choosing a completion date, and read the Portuguese half of the residency question in the [183-day guide](/guides/portugal-tax-residency-183-days/) alongside it.

## What does the currency pair do to a Canadian file?

Every Canadian filing about a Portuguese property has to be made in Canadian dollars, and every document the property generates is in euros. That translation is not difficult and it is relentless, and it produces a category of error that has nothing to do with tax law.

The cost amount is fixed in Canadian dollars at acquisition, using the rate on that date, and it does not move afterwards. Income and expenses are translated as they arise. The Portuguese tax paid is translated for the credit. A gain on sale is computed in Canadian dollars from a Canadian-dollar cost base, which means the Canadian gain and the Portuguese gain can differ substantially even when the euro price barely moved.

Four consequences worth building into the file from year 1:

- **Keep the acquisition-date rate with the deed.** In 10 years nobody will remember it, and reconstructing a historic rate is possible but tedious.
- **Record income monthly rather than annually**, because an average annual rate is a simplification the CRA may or may not accept for a given item.
- **Expect the 2 gains to differ.** A property that rose 15% in euros can show a larger or smaller Canadian gain depending entirely on the currency pair over the same 10 years.
- **The credit is limited by the Canadian tax on that income**, so a year with heavy Portuguese tax and light Canadian tax does not produce full relief.

The [currency transfer guide](/guides/currency-transfer-portugal-property/) deals with moving money efficiently at purchase; the point here is the quieter one, that the exchange rate becomes a permanent input to a Canadian tax file for as long as the property is held.

## Which Portuguese decisions actually matter to a Canadian?

Most of the Portuguese purchase is identical for every nationality. Three choices interact specifically with the Canadian position and are worth taking deliberately.

| Portuguese decision | Why it matters on the Canadian side |
|---|---|
| Whether to let at all | Flips the property between 2 reporting regimes |
| Long tenancy or short-stay | Changes the income profile and the record-keeping load |
| Sole or joint ownership | Determines whose Canadian return carries it |
| Timing of completion | Interacts with any planned change of residence |
| Whether improvements are documented | Both gain calculations depend on the same receipts |
| Whether a company is used | Replaces the personal-use test with corporate rules |

The [long-term versus holiday letting guide](/guides/long-term-vs-holiday-rental-portugal/) covers the Portuguese economics of the second row. What that guide cannot tell a Canadian is that the choice also determines how much of their year is spent on compliance, which for a single property is the difference between an afternoon and a weekend, every year, for the life of the holding.

## How a Canadian file looks across a ten-year hold

Setting the years out end to end shows where the effort actually falls, which is not where buyers expect.

| Period | What the Canadian file contains |
|---|---|
| Purchase year | Cost amount fixed in Canadian dollars; no filing if personal use |
| Years 1 to 5, personal use | Nothing beyond keeping records |
| First year of letting | T1135 begins; rental income enters the Canadian return |
| Subsequent letting years | Annual filing, annual translation, annual credit claim |
| Year of any move | Deemed disposition on ceasing residence |
| Sale year | Canadian gain computed from the Canadian-dollar cost base |

The shape of that table is the argument. For 5 years an owner who does not let has essentially no Canadian administration and can be forgiven for thinking there is none. The load arrives all at once, in the first year of letting, and it arrives with a retrospective element because the cost amount and the improvement history have to be produced from records that may not have been kept.

That is why the single most valuable habit for a Canadian buyer is unglamorous: a folder, started at the deed, holding the purchase documents, the exchange rate on the acquisition date, and every improvement invoice. It costs an hour a year and it is the difference between a straightforward filing and an expensive reconstruction 6 years later.

## Pros, cons and the checklist

The advantages for Canadians are straightforward and often understated. There is no citizenship-based taxation to carry, so a move genuinely changes the position. The letting decision is optional rather than forced. And Portugal treats a Canadian buyer identically to any other non-resident, so the Portuguese side of the purchase is the standard one described in the [foreign buyer's guide](/guides/buy-property-portugal-foreigner/).

The disadvantages are administrative rather than fiscal. The reporting switch is invisible until it flips. Currency adds a translation layer to every figure, which the [currency transfer guide](/guides/currency-transfer-portugal-property/) covers on the transaction side and which persists in every annual filing afterwards. And the departure rules mean a future intention has a present cost.

**Checklist before the CPCV:**

1. Decide honestly whether the property will be let, because that single answer determines the reporting regime.
2. If letting is possible later, set up records from day 1 that would support either position.
3. Record the cost amount in Canadian dollars at the acquisition date, and keep the conversion evidence.
4. Establish whether relocation is on the horizon, and if so, discuss the deemed disposition before fixing the completion date.
5. Keep improvement receipts in a form that survives 10 years, for both the Canadian and the Portuguese gain calculations, the latter set out in the [mais-valias guide](/guides/mais-valias-portugal-capital-gains/).
6. If short-let is intended, read the [Alojamento Local rules](/guides/alojamento-local-license-portugal/) before assuming the property qualifies.

One further asymmetry is worth naming, because it catches families who own on both sides of the Atlantic. Canada taxes its residents on worldwide income and Portugal taxes non-residents only on Portuguese-source income, so while the family remains Canadian the Portuguese house is a small part of a Canadian return. If the family later becomes Portuguese resident, the direction reverses: the Canadian assets become the foreign ones, reported to Portugal, and the Portuguese house becomes domestic. Nothing about the property changes in either direction; the reporting perimeter moves around it. Households that expect to make that move eventually should keep both files to the same standard from the beginning, because whichever country ends up as home will ask about the other one.

Insider tip: the year you first take rent is the year your file changes character, and nobody tells you at the time. Diarise it. A Canadian owner who knows the exact date the property stopped being personal-use has a straightforward conversation with their accountant; one who does not has an expensive reconstruction.
