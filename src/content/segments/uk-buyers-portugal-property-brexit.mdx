## Brexit changed the calendar, not the deed

British buyers arrive at Portuguese property with a specific anxiety, and it is usually aimed at the wrong target. The worry is about ownership: whether a Briton can still buy, whether there is a permit, whether the rules changed at the notary. They did not. Portugal does not restrict residential ownership by nationality, the escritura is the same document, the registry treats a British owner exactly as it treats a Dutch one, and the [foreign buyer's guide](/guides/buy-property-portugal-foreigner/) applies without a British footnote.

What ended was free movement. Before 2021 a British owner could spend as much of the year in an Algarve house as they wished, and many built their lives around that. After it, the same owner holds the same title with a visitor's clock attached to it.

That is the whole of the Brexit effect on this market, and it is enough. A property you may own but occupy for a limited number of days is a different asset from the one many British owners thought they had bought, and the difference shows up in how the property is used, whether it is let, and eventually in who buys it.

## How the count actually works

The rule is stated simply and applied in a way that catches people out. A third-country national may spend up to 90 days in any rolling 180-day period inside the Schengen area.

Four features do the damage:

- **It rolls.** There is no reset date. On any day you look back over the previous 180 days and add up what you have used, so the allowance returns gradually as old days fall out of the window.
- **It is area-wide.** Days in Spain, France or anywhere else in the Schengen area count against the same 90, so a driving holiday reduces the time available at the Portuguese house.
- **Part days count whole.** The day you arrive and the day you leave are each a full day, so a long weekend costs 4 days rather than 2 nights.
- **It applies per person.** Couples travelling on different schedules have different balances, which is a common source of a surprise at a border for the partner who visited more often.

The arithmetic that follows is unforgiving in one particular pattern: an owner who uses the full 90 days in the first half of the year has nothing available for the second half until the window turns over. Long summers and Christmas are not automatically compatible.

## What machine-kept borders change

The EU's Entry/Exit System registers third-country nationals' entries and exits electronically instead of by passport stamp. It does not change the 90/180 rule at all. It changes who knows the number.

| Before | Under electronic registration |
|---|---|
| Count reconstructed from stamps | Count held in a database |
| Missed or illegible stamps created ambiguity | No ambiguity to rely on |
| Owner's own record was the working figure | The system's record is the working figure |
| Overstays often surfaced at the next entry | Position is visible at every crossing |
| Informal discretion at quiet borders | Consistent application |
| Errors argued case by case | Errors corrected against a record |

The honest summary is that nothing about the entitlement has been reduced and everything about its measurement has tightened. Owners whose practice was approximately compliant become precisely compliant or precisely not, and the group most affected is not the deliberate overstayer but the owner who has never actually counted.

## The routes that buy back time

Property ownership confers no immigration right in Portugal. This bears repeating because it is the most persistent misunderstanding in the British segment: a deed is not a permit, and no amount of money spent on a house changes the 90 days.

Time comes from a visa, and the choice is a genuine trade rather than a formality:

1. **Stay a visitor.** No application, no Portuguese tax residency, 90 days in each rolling 180. Suits owners whose use is genuinely seasonal.
2. **Take a residence visa.** Removes the Schengen limit and makes you resident, which brings Portuguese tax residency and its worldwide scope with it. The [183-day residency guide](/guides/portugal-tax-residency-183-days/) sets out where that line sits, and the [residency options guide](/guides/portugal-residency-options-without-golden-visa/) covers the routes that remain open.
3. **A passive-income route** such as the D7, which suits retired and independently funded owners and has its own income and accommodation requirements, covered in the [D7 guide](/guides/portugal-d7-visa-property/).

Each route has to be applied for from the UK, in advance, with lead times measured in months. That ordering matters more than anything else on this page: a buyer who completes in March and starts thinking about a visa in April has already lost the summer.

## What the limit does to the property you choose

The time constraint should change the purchase, and for most British buyers it does not, which is where the value is lost.

Buyer scenario. A couple buy a 3-bedroom villa for family summers, planning 12 weeks a year, about 3 months, across 2 visits. That is 84 days, inside the allowance, with almost nothing left for a Christmas trip. The house then stands empty for roughly 9 months, carrying IMI, condominium charges, insurance and maintenance for a use of about 23% of the 12 months.

The same couple, on the same budget, buying a smaller apartment in a lettable location, use it for the same 84 days and let it for a further 4 months. The running costs are covered, the property is occupied and maintained, and the exit pool is wider because the flat suits both owner-occupiers and investors.

Neither choice is wrong. But the first is a lifestyle decision with a cost that the 90-day rule makes considerably larger than it was before 2021, and it should be taken deliberately rather than by default.

## Counting a real year, month by month

The rule reads as an abstraction until it is applied to an actual pattern of visits. Here is a year that looks reasonable and is not.

| Visit | Days used | Running total in the window |
|---|---|---|
| February half term, 9 days | 9 | 9 |
| Easter, 16 days | 16 | 25 |
| Late May, 10 days | 10 | 35 |
| July and August, 45 days | 45 | 80 |
| October, 12 days | 12 | 92 — over |
| Christmas, 14 days | 14 | not available |

The October trip is where this owner crosses the line, and almost nobody notices in advance because each individual visit looks modest. The summer block is what does the damage: 45 consecutive days consumes half the annual allowance and stays in the rolling window for the following 6 months.

Two adjustments fix it without giving anything up that matters. Splitting the summer into 2 shorter stays releases days later in the year, and moving the October trip into the following window turns an overstay into a compliant year. Both decisions have to be made about 4 months ahead, in March, which is the practical reason to keep the count from the first visit rather than reconstruct it in the autumn.

## Who this affects most, and who it barely touches

Not every British owner is equally exposed, and the differences follow use rather than wealth.

- **Barely affected: the investor who never visits.** A let property with 2 inspection trips a year uses a fortnight and nothing else. The Schengen count is irrelevant to the asset.
- **Barely affected: the short-break owner.** Four visits of 10 days is 40 days, comfortably inside the allowance with room for a fifth.
- **Squeezed: the long-summer family.** The pattern in the table above, which is the traditional British second-home rhythm and the one Brexit changed most.
- **Blocked: the near-resident.** An owner who wants 6 months a year has no route through the visitor rules at all and must take a residence permit or reduce their use by half.

The last group is the one where the property decision and the immigration decision genuinely have to be taken together, because the answer changes what should be bought. A person who will become resident should think about the Portuguese tax year from the outset; a person who will stay a visitor should size the property to 90 days of use and a letting plan for the rest.

## What actually changed on the money side, and what did not

Two changes are real and one widely believed change is not.

- **Fiscal representation.** British owners became non-EU for this purpose, so a representative is required unless the electronic-notifications route is used. The [fiscal representative guide](/guides/fiscal-representative-portugal/) covers both.
- **Mortgage terms.** Portuguese banks distinguish third-country borrowers from EU-resident ones, and the non-resident products differ in loan-to-value and pricing, as set out in the [non-resident mortgage guide](/guides/non-resident-mortgage-portugal/).
- **Acquisition tax did not change with Brexit.** IMT treatment turns on tax residency, not on nationality, so a British buyer and a French buyer in the same position pay the same.

On the ongoing tax side, the 1968 UK-Portugal double taxation convention continues to govern how income and gains are allocated between the two countries, by the credit method rather than by exemption. The [treaty guide](/guides/uk-portugal-double-taxation-treaty/) sets out the mechanics; Brexit did not alter it, because it is a bilateral treaty rather than an EU instrument.

## What this does to resale, over a 10 years view

The time limit does not only change how a British owner uses a property. It changes who will buy it from them, and that effect compounds quietly.

The traditional British Algarve resale market ran on other British buyers, many of whom wanted the same long-summer pattern the rules now restrict. That pool has not disappeared, but it has thinned at exactly the end where the biggest houses sit, because a 4-bedroom villa is hardest to justify for a use of 90 days a year. Meanwhile the buyer pools that are unaffected — EU nationals with free movement, and investors who never intended to occupy — value different things: lettability, running costs, proximity to services rather than to a particular beach.

The practical instruction for a British buyer today is to check whether the property they are choosing suits at least 2 of those pools rather than only the shrinking one:

- **Suits British second-home buyers**, which is the pool the rules squeezed.
- **Suits EU buyers**, who face no time limit and price on lifestyle and location.
- **Suits investors**, who price on yield, running costs and the letting calendar.
- **Suits eventual residents**, including the buyer themselves if a visa route is taken later.

A property that only works for the first is a bet that the pool recovers. A property that works for 3 of the 4 is not making that bet at all, and over 10 years of ownership that is worth considerably more than the €20,000 or €30,000 of negotiation most buyers concentrate on at the point of offer.

**Checklist, in the order that matters:**

1. Decide the intended annual use in days, honestly, before choosing a property type.
2. If that number exceeds 90, start the visa route before the property search, not after the offer.
3. Keep your own record of crossings from the first visit, so that the database and your account agree.
4. Settle the fiscal representation position at the same time as the NIF.
5. If financing, obtain a decision in principle as a third-country borrower before making offers.
6. Read the treaty position on any rental income before letting, rather than in the following April.

Red flag: an agent who says the 90 days "is not really enforced". That may have described the stamped era; it does not describe an electronically recorded one, and a buyer making a 6-figure decision on the strength of it is relying on a gap that is being closed. Buyers from countries whose own tax system follows them regardless of residence face a different problem entirely — see the [American page](/segments/american-buyers-portugal-property/) — and the two situations should not be reasoned about together.
