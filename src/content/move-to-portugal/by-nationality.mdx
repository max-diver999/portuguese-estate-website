Quick Answer: Nationality does not change your right to buy in Portugal or what Portugal charges you. It changes what your home country does with the asset afterwards. This page covers eleven countries and the one thing that genuinely differs for each, with the shared Portuguese process stated once rather than eleven times.

Portugal is unusually simple on the question everyone asks first. There is no nationality condition on residential property, no approval process for foreign buyers, and no separate register. An Angolan, an Australian and a German take the same freehold, at the same notary, with the same protections.

Everything that actually differs happens somewhere else: in the tax system of the country you are resident in, and in the banking system of the country your money is leaving. That is what this page maps.

## What Is the Same for Everyone

| Step | What it is | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| NIF | Portuguese tax number | Nothing proceeds without it, including the bank account |
| Portuguese bank account | For the transfer and the ongoing charges | Source-of-funds diligence applies to every nationality |
| Independent lawyer | Title, encumbrances, licensing | The agent is paid by the seller, not by you |
| CPCV promissory contract | Binding, with a deposit | The deposit is at risk under the double-the-deposit rule |
| Escritura at the notary | The deed and registration | Ownership transfers here, not at the CPCV |

And the Portuguese charges, which do not vary by passport:

| Charge | Basis |
|---|---|
| IMT, non-resident buyer | Flat 7.5% on residential property from 1 September 2026 under DL 97/2026 |
| Stamp duty | 0.8% of the purchase price |
| IMI | 0.3% to 0.45% of rateable value annually for urban property, set by each council |
| AIMI | 0.7% on taxable value above EUR 600,000 per owner |
| Stamp duty on gratuitous transfers | 10%, with spouse, descendants and ascendants exempt |

The sequence is set out in [buying property in Portugal as a foreigner](/guides/buy-property-portugal-foreigner/), the tax number in [the NIF guide](/guides/nif-portugal-property-purchase/), and the representative requirement in [fiscal representative in Portugal](/guides/fiscal-representative-portugal/).

Buyers from the United States and the United Kingdom have their own pages, because both markets carry enough demand to deserve one: [moving to Portugal from the US](/move-to-portugal/from-usa/) and [moving to Portugal from the UK](/move-to-portugal/from-uk/).

## Germany: the Inheritance Nobody Warns You About

Two countries reach a German family's Portuguese house at death, and they reach it in opposite directions.

Portugal abolished inheritance tax and replaced it with stamp duty on gratuitous transfers, which exempts transfers to a spouse, to descendants and to ascendants. For the ordinary case, a house passing to a widow or to children, the Portuguese charge is nil. A Portuguese adviser will tell you, correctly, that there is no inheritance tax.

Germany taxes the same inheritance, and there is no bilateral inheritance tax treaty between the two countries to relieve it. The sentence "there is no inheritance tax in Portugal" is true and, for a German family, close to worthless.

This is a structuring question to settle before the purchase, not after a death. The Portuguese side is in [inheritance tax on Portuguese property](/guides/portugal-property-inheritance-tax-foreigners/); the German side needs German advice.

## France: One House, Two Wealth Computations

Most foreign owners think about annual property taxes once: IMI, plus AIMI if the holding is large enough.

A French tax resident thinks about them twice, because France assesses the impôt sur la fortune immobilière on worldwide real estate. The same Algarve villa sits simultaneously in a French annual computation and a Portuguese one. Neither country is doing anything unusual, and the interaction is the point.

The second French-specific trap is the SCI reflex. Holding French property through a société civile immobilière is standard practice at home and is frequently exported to Portugal without checking what it does to the Portuguese position. It can cost money rather than save it.

## Canada: the Threshold That Is Not the Rule

Canadian owners consistently expect a threshold, and they get one: specified foreign property triggers a T1135 filing above a cost amount of CAD 100,000. That sounds like the whole rule.

It is not, because the definition carves out property held primarily for personal use and enjoyment. A holiday home used by the family is exactly that, and a EUR 600,000 apartment can sit outside the filing requirement while a cheaper one that is let does not.

The line moves with use, not with value. The year you first take rent is the year your file changes character, and nobody tells you at the time. Diarise it.

## United Arab Emirates: the Credit That Does Not Exist

The standard logic for a cross-border owner has two steps. Portugal taxes the Portuguese income; your home country taxes worldwide income and credits what Portugal took.

For a UAE-resident individual, step two is missing. There is no personal income tax at home, so there is no bill to reduce and no credit to claim. The Portuguese tax is not a prepayment of anything. It is the price, and it is final.

The practical consequence is that effort spent minimising Portuguese tax pays a UAE resident directly, in a way it does not pay a British or German owner who would simply get less credit at home. This is the one cohort for whom Portuguese structuring is genuinely worth its cost.

## India: the Constraint Is the Transfer, Not the Title

Portugal allows Indian citizens to buy residential freehold with no citizenship, residency or approval requirement. The purchase is not the constraint.

The Reserve Bank of India's Liberalised Remittance Scheme is. It caps what an individual may remit abroad in a financial year, which shapes how a purchase is funded and over what period, particularly for a family pooling across several remitters. Plan the transfer schedule before the promissory contract, because the CPCV deadline will not wait for the next financial year.

Indian buyers also feature in the visa cluster: `portugal visa for indians` runs at about 50 searches a month in both the US and UK databases, which is small but real.

## China: Capital Controls and SWIFT Friction

Chinese nationals buy into modern lift-served apartments in Lisbon's Avenidas Novas, the riverfront at Parque das Nações, and renovated stock in central Porto. The ticket profile is different from the lifestyle buyer cohorts.

The friction is entirely on the transfer side: capital controls at home and correspondent banking delays in between. Portuguese banks apply full source-of-funds diligence, and the documentation chain from a Chinese source to a Portuguese notary is longer than any other on this page. Build the timeline backwards from the deed date.

Non-EU residence also triggers the fiscal representative requirement.

## Brazil: the Largest Foreign Cohort in the Market

Brazilians are not a niche in Portugal, they are the market leader. INE residential transaction data for 2025 records 9,808 purchases by Brazilian-born buyers, up 27.5% year on year and ahead of every other foreign nationality.

The distinction that matters within that number is domicile. INE counted 34,838 transactions involving foreign-born buyers holding Portuguese tax domicile, up 11.4%. A Brazilian buyer already resident and tax domiciled in Portugal is not a non-resident purchaser: they sit on the resident IMT scale, not the flat 7.5%, and the whole cost model changes.

Establish which of the two you are before modelling the purchase. It is the single largest variable for this cohort.

## Israel: Holding a Euro Asset Outside Israel

For Israeli buyers the question is rarely whether they can buy. It is how to hold a euro-denominated asset outside Israel, how the rental income is treated on both sides, and whether the property is a base or an investment.

Not every buyer is emigrating. A meaningful share are diversifying geographically while remaining Israeli tax resident, which puts them squarely in the non-resident bracket for Portuguese purposes, at the flat 7.5% IMT rate and with rental income taxed at source.

Search behaviour for this cohort is largely in Hebrew, which an English-language page cannot serve well. That is a language question rather than a content one.

## South Africa: Exchange Control and Remote Completion

South African buyers compare euro property against rand assets, offshore allowances and family education plans. The legal position is straightforward: a South African passport blocks nothing.

The practical constraints are exchange control and distance. Moving rand into euros happens within an allowance framework that has to be planned rather than improvised, and most of these purchases complete remotely from Johannesburg or Cape Town through a power of attorney. Both are solvable; neither is quick.

## Angola: Lusophone Ties and Enhanced Diligence

Five centuries of connection, post-independence migration and a steady flow of Angolan professionals into Lisbon and Porto make Portugal a natural destination, and Angolan capital concentrates in those two cities at premium price tiers rather than in the coastal resort markets.

The friction is compliance. Portuguese banks apply enhanced anti-money-laundering diligence to Angolan transfers, which means a longer, better-documented source-of-funds chain than any European buyer faces. Nothing about that is an obstacle to a legitimate purchase, but it is a timeline item, and treating it as a formality is how deadlines get missed.

## Australia: Distance Changes the Use Case

Australians rarely make a quick weekend-home purchase in Portugal. The flight distance and time zone gap mean it competes against a holiday house at home rather than against Spain or France.

That changes the property. The Algarve offers a climate and outdoor life familiar to Australians at coastal price points below Sydney, Melbourne or Perth, but a house visited twice a year needs management, not just purchase. Currency is the other long-run variable: AUD to EUR exposure over a ten or fifteen year hold is larger than any negotiation on price.

## Pros and Cons of Buying as a Foreign National

Advantages:

- No nationality restriction, no approval process, no separate register
- Freehold title with the same protections a Portuguese buyer receives
- A mature conveyancing process with independent legal representation as the norm
- Non-resident mortgage lending available from Portuguese banks
- An IMT refund route if you later become Portuguese tax resident within 24 months

Disadvantages:

- Flat 7.5% IMT while you remain non-resident, against a lower resident scale
- Fiscal representative required for non-EU and non-EEA residents
- Transfer constraints in several jurisdictions that have nothing to do with Portugal
- Home-country treatment of the asset varies enormously and is rarely modelled before purchase
- Currency exposure across the whole holding period, not just at completion

## Risks and Red Flags

- **"There is no inheritance tax in Portugal."** True, and for a German family it is the most expensive true sentence in this market.
- **A single adviser covering both jurisdictions.** Almost nobody is genuinely qualified on both sides. Use two and make them talk.
- **Exporting a home-country structure.** The French SCI is the clearest example, and it is not the only one.
- **Treating a transfer constraint as a formality.** Indian LRS, Chinese controls, South African exchange control and Angolan diligence are all timeline items with deadlines attached.
- **Assuming your domicile.** A Brazilian buyer already tax domiciled in Portugal is on a different IMT scale from one who is not, and the difference is five figures.

Checklist for any nationality:

- Confirm whether you are buying as a Portuguese tax resident or a non-resident, in writing
- Get a home-country opinion on holding, letting and passing on the asset, before the CPCV
- Map the transfer timeline against the promissory contract deadline
- Appoint a fiscal representative if you are resident outside the EU or EEA
- Decide the currency approach before the deposit, covered in [currency transfer for a Portugal property purchase](/guides/currency-transfer-portugal-property/)

## Which Question Applies to You

**If your home country taxes worldwide income and gives credit.** Most European and Commonwealth buyers. Portuguese tax is largely a prepayment, and the effort belongs in the home-country filing rather than in Portuguese structuring.

**If your home country taxes on citizenship.** United States buyers. The saving clause changes the whole analysis: see [moving to Portugal from the US](/move-to-portugal/from-usa/).

**If your home country taxes nothing.** UAE residents. Portuguese tax is final, and minimising it is the only lever that pays.

**If your constraint is moving the money.** India, China, South Africa, Angola. The Portuguese side is easy and the transfer is the project. Start it before you find the house.
