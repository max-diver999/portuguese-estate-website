Quick Answer: in Lagos, water is not a running cost. It is a planning input, and it decides whether a pool fills, whether a garden survives, and whether a plot receives the connection a build programme was priced around.

## Water reaches a buyer through permissions, not through the bill

The usual way a foreign buyer thinks about water in southern Europe is as a utility: a meter, a tariff, a slightly higher summer bill. In the Algarve that framing hides the mechanism that actually matters. When storage falls, the response is not primarily a price signal, it is an administrative one. Contingency measures are adopted at national level by resolution, translated into targets and restrictions by the regional water authorities, and applied on the ground through the municipal or intermunicipal operator.

What arrives at an individual property is therefore a rule rather than an invoice. Non-essential uses can be restricted, and the categories that recur in those instruments are precisely the ones a second-home buyer cares about: filling private swimming pools, irrigating gardens and green space, washing paved areas and vehicles. The restrictions carry exemptions and thresholds, and they change as conditions change, which is the part buyers find hardest to price.

The consequence is a clean distinction that cuts through most Lagos listings, and it sorts the market into 3 exposures rather than 1:

- **An apartment with no pool and an existing connection** is barely exposed at all. The regime reaches it as a tariff and nothing more.
- **A villa bought substantially for its pool and planted grounds** is exposed in a specific, seasonal, recurring way, across the 5 months when the amenity is the reason the property was bought, against 7 months when it is barely used.
- **A plot bought to build on** is exposed differently again, because its exposure is to timing rather than to amenity, and timing compounds across a build programme.

## What does the reservoir number actually tell you?

The Barragem da Bravura sits near Lagos and its storage percentage is published and reported, which is why it becomes the number in every conversation about Algarve water. It is a legitimate indicator and it is worth watching. It is not a supply diagram.

Algarve water supply is managed across multiple sources at regional scale, and which source physically serves a given address is a question for the operator's supply mapping rather than for a property page. Writing as though a particular tap draws from a particular dam is the kind of confident detail that reads well and misleads a buyer into checking the wrong thing. The honest position is narrower and more useful: the published storage series tells you what pressure the system is under, and the pressure determines whether restriction instruments are in force, and it is the instruments that reach your property.

So the sequence to follow has 3 steps, in this order:

1. **Read the published level as a signal**, not as a verdict on your address.
2. **Read the measures actually in force**, from the instruments rather than from coverage of them, because the exemptions matter as much as the restrictions.
3. **Ask the operator about the specific address**, in writing, since only they can answer for the connection you are buying.

## Where the risk actually sits in a transaction

| What you are buying | Exposure to the water regime | The question to ask before the CPCV |
|---|---|---|
| Apartment, existing connection, no pool | Minimal, essentially tariff only | Nothing beyond standard title checks |
| Apartment in a block with a shared pool | Shared amenity may be restricted seasonally | Condominium minutes on how restrictions were handled |
| Villa with an existing licensed pool | Refilling and irrigation may be restricted | Whether the pool and its supply are documented and licensed |
| Villa bought to add a pool | Both the works licence and the water position | Câmara on the licence, operator on the supply |
| Plot for a single dwelling | Connection capacity and timing | Written confirmation of connection feasibility |
| Subdivision or conversion adding units | Highest: capacity, timing, and infrastructure cost | Operator's requirements in writing, before any deposit |

The pattern in that table is the argument of the page. Exposure rises with how much new demand the purchase creates. A buyer stepping into an existing dwelling with an existing connection inherits a position the system already accommodates. A buyer creating new demand is asking the system for something, in a region that has spent recent years being asked for more than it comfortably has.

## Why does scarcity support the stock that already exists?

It is tempting to read all of this as a reason to avoid the market, and the arithmetic points the other way for most buyers. A constraint on new supply is, by definition, protection for supply that already exists. A villa with a licensed pool, a mature garden and a settled connection is a harder thing to replicate in a constrained regime than it was in an unconstrained one, and that difficulty is worth something on resale.

Lagos compounds this from 2 other directions that have nothing to do with water. The historic centre inside the old walls has very little room for new building, and the cliff coastline west of the town is protected, so outward expansion in the directions buyers most want is limited by planning rather than by demand. Add a water regime that makes new development slower and more conditional, and the result is a market where the scarce thing is finished, connected, licensed stock.

That is a genuine investment case. It is also a precise one, and it does not extend to every property in the municipality. It applies to what is already built and legal, and it argues against paying a premium for potential that depends on permissions nobody has yet granted.

## Which questions does the water regime actually answer for you?

The regime is easier to use once you stop treating it as a risk to be feared and start treating it as a set of answers you can obtain before committing. Each of the 6 rows below is obtainable within 30 days, most of it inside 10 days, and all of it before a deposit moves.

| What you want to know | Who answers it | Why it is not the seller |
|---|---|---|
| Is there a connection, and is it live? | The water operator | The seller knows the tap works, not the contract behind it |
| Can this plot get a new connection? | The water operator, in writing | Agents routinely assume feasibility from a neighbour's supply |
| What restrictions apply this season? | The instruments in force | Coverage reports the headline, not the exemptions |
| Is the pool licensed as built? | The câmara file | A pool that exists is not the same as a pool that is documented |
| Were works ever done without permission? | The câmara file plus the registry | An as-built gap travels with the property, not the seller |
| What happened here in the last restricted year? | The seller and the neighbours | It is the one thing only they can tell you |

The last row is the one to spend time on. Owners who have been through a restricted season remember it precisely: what they could and could not do, what the garden looked like by September, whether the pool was drained. That recollection is worth more than any percentage, because it describes the asset you are buying under the conditions that will recur.

## Two buyers on the same street

Buyer scenario, and it is the clearest way to see why a single price per square metre describes neither party. Two purchasers agree on similar villas on the same road outside Lagos in the same month, at similar prices.

The first buys a house completed 12 years ago, roughly 18 months after the estate around it was finished, with a licensed pool, a mature garden on an established irrigation system, and a water connection that has been in place since construction. Everything that needs a permission already has one. When restrictions come into force, the household changes its behaviour for a season: the pool is topped up rather than drained and refilled, the garden is watered on the permitted schedule, and the year costs a little more attention and no capital.

The second buys the neighbouring plot intending to build, with a pool in the design and a garden in the render. Nothing that needs a permission has one yet. Before a single wall goes up there are 2 separate approvals to obtain from 2 separate bodies: the câmara for the works, the operator for the supply. Each has its own queue, and the second can be affected by measures the first knows nothing about. If either slips, the build slips, and a build that slips through a winter into a second season carries the financing cost of the delay.

Neither buyer made a mistake. They bought different assets at the same price, and only one of them was priced on permissions already granted. That is the whole Lagos argument compressed into one street.

## The acquisition arithmetic, and where it is national rather than local

Purchase costs are national and are set out in full in the [cost of buying guide](/guides/cost-of-buying-property-portugal/); the non-resident rate and the residency refund route sit in the [IMT guide](/guides/imt-tax-non-resident-portugal-2026/). Neither is repeated here, because neither is a Lagos question.

What is local is how the model should be shaped around them:

- **Model outdoor amenity as a range, not a line.** Pool and irrigation costs move with the measures in force, so a single annual figure will be wrong in both directions across a hold period.
- **On a plot or a conversion, carry a timing contingency rather than a cost one.** The risk here is a programme that waits on 2 separate approvals, not a bill that grows.
- **Price the permission, not the render.** A design with a pool in it is worth what the pool is worth once licensed and connected, which is a different number before those exist.
- **Treat a restricted season as a normal event over 10 years of ownership, not an exception.** A model that assumes unrestricted years throughout will overstate the outdoor amenity it is paying for.

Short-let strategy is likewise a national regime with a municipal overlay, covered in the [Alojamento Local guide](/guides/alojamento-local-license-portugal/). What is local is only this: a property whose summer appeal rests on outdoor amenity carries more variance in a restricted year than one whose appeal is a walkable location and a sea view.

## Strengths, weaknesses and the buyer this suits

The strengths are the ones above plus the obvious: a working town rather than a resort, a marina, a long season, and a beach coastline that carries its own demand without a single operator behind it. Rental demand runs roughly 8 months from spring through autumn, and the town keeps a resident population through the remaining 4 months, which supports 12 months of long-term letting in a way that pure resort addresses do not.

The weaknesses are specific rather than general. Any purchase whose value rests on a pool, a garden or a future build carries a conditional element the buyer does not control. Renovation adds a second layer of permission on top of the first, and the [obras licence guide](/guides/obras-licence-portugal-renovation/) covers where that clock runs. And prices in the historic centre reflect scarcity that is already understood by the market, which limits the room for the value argument buyers arrive hoping to make.

This market suits a buyer purchasing something finished. It suits a buyer who intends to hold rather than to improve and flip, because improvements here run through more permissions than in an unconstrained market. It suits worst the buyer who has priced a plot on the assumption that everything is a formality.

**Before you commit, in this order:**

1. Written confirmation from the water operator on connection status or feasibility for the exact address, obtained before the deposit rather than during the CPCV window.
2. The measures currently in force, read from the instruments themselves rather than from press coverage of them.
3. For any pool: evidence it is licensed and documented, not merely present and full.
4. For any works: the câmara position on the licence, since a permission and a supply are two different approvals and both can fail.
5. Standard title pack through a Portuguese lawyer, per the [due diligence guide](/guides/due-diligence-portugal-property/).
6. Running-cost modelling that treats irrigation and pool maintenance as variable and conditional, not fixed, alongside the [maintenance cost guide](/guides/property-maintenance-costs-portugal/).

Insider tip: the seller of a villa with a pool will tell you the pool is fine. The useful question is not whether it is fine, but what happened to it during the last period of restrictions, and who paid for the answer. Owners remember that year in detail, and the answer tells you what you are actually buying. For how the same coastline prices differently a few municipalities east, where the constraint is tourist-development title rather than water, read [Portimão](/areas/portimao-property-investment/).
