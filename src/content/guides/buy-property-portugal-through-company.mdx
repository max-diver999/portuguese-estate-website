  - question: "Can a foreign buyer own Portuguese property through a company?"
    answer: "Yes, through a Portuguese Lda, another EU company or a non-EU company, with no nationality restriction on the shareholders. The structure is legal and common; whether it saves money is a separate calculation that usually favours personal ownership below three properties."
  - question: "What tax does a Portuguese Lda pay on rental income?"
    answer: "IRC at the standard rate of around 20%, with a reduced 16% on the first €50,000 of taxable profit for qualifying SMEs, computed after deducting depreciation, interest, management and maintenance. Extracting the profit as dividends then costs a further 28% withholding for individual shareholders, before any treaty relief."
  - question: "What does personal ownership pay on the same rent?"
    answer: "25% autonomous tax on long-let rent after limited deductions: condominium charges, IMI, insurance and documented maintenance, but not mortgage interest. No second layer applies because the net rent is already yours, which is the structural advantage personal ownership holds for small portfolios."
  - question: "How does AIMI treat companies differently?"
    answer: "Companies pay AIMI at 0.4% on the full taxable value of residential holdings with no €600,000 allowance; individuals pay 0.7% to 1.5% but only above €600,000 per owner. Below roughly €600,000 of VPT the individual pays zero and the company pays every year."
  - question: "What is the blacklist penalty on corporate ownership?"
    answer: "Property held through entities in listed tax havens faces punitive rates: IMT at 10% on acquisition, annual IMI at 7.5%, and AIMI at 7.5%. Structures routed through zero-tax jurisdictions stopped making sense for Portuguese property years ago, and unwinding them costs a full transfer."
  - question: "Does a company avoid Portuguese capital gains tax on sale?"
    answer: "No. A Portuguese company pays IRC on the full gain with no 50% exclusion and no inflation coefficient, though reinvestment relief can halve the taxed portion if proceeds are fully reinvested in qualifying assets. Selling the shares instead of the property is possible but the buyer pool for shares is far smaller."
  - question: "When does a company structure actually make sense?"
    answer: "From roughly three properties, where shared accounting overhead spreads, interest deductibility matters, and profits are reinvested inside the company rather than extracted. Also for genuine development or AL operation run as a business, and for some succession plans built around share transfers."
  - question: "What does running a Portuguese Lda cost each year?"
    answer: "Certified accounting is mandatory and runs €1,500 to €3,000 a year for a simple property company, plus bank charges and filing obligations. Incorporation via Empresa na Hora costs €360. The overhead is fixed, which is exactly why it needs several properties to justify itself."
  - question: "Is inheritance easier with a company?"
    answer: "Share transfers can simplify cross-border succession mechanics, and stamp duty exemptions between spouses, descendants and ascendants apply to Portuguese assets either way. For non-close heirs, shares in a property company do not escape the economic effect of the 10% stamp duty planning question; specialist advice is essential."
  - question: "Can I move my existing property into a company later?"
    answer: "Only by a full transfer that triggers IMT, stamp duty and potentially capital gains tax at that date, which typically costs 8% to 12% of the property's value. The structure decision is effectively made at purchase; retrofitting it is the most expensive way to reach the same place."

Structure questions come to every foreign buyer from an adviser, a forum or a neighbour, usually phrased as though a company were a tax switch waiting to be flipped. The honest arithmetic is narrower. Portugal taxes personal landlords simply, 25% on long-let rent, half the capital gain at progressive rates on exit, and taxes companies twice, around 20% inside the structure and 28% again at the shareholder's door. A company wins where its deductions are large, its overhead is shared across several properties and its profits stay inside to compound; it loses where a single apartment carries €2,000 of fixed accounting against a few thousand euros of annual rent. The break-even sits near three properties for most files, and the decision is effectively permanent, because moving a property into or out of a company later is a full taxable transfer costing 8% to 12% of its value.

## What are you actually comparing?

Ownership comparison in Portugal is a two-layer question: what each structure pays yearly on rent, and what each pays once at exit, because the winner of the annual round frequently loses the exit round — in the worked example below, by €47,000 on a single apartment. Foreign buyers who model only the rental year choose companies too often.

| Layer | Personal | Portuguese Lda |
|---|---|---|
| Rental tax | 25% autonomous on net rent | ~20% IRC, 16% on first €50,000 for SMEs |
| Deductions | Condominium, IMI, insurance, maintenance | All business costs, interest, depreciation |
| Mortgage interest | Not deductible | Deductible |
| Extraction | None needed, rent is yours | 28% withholding on dividends |
| Capital gain at sale | 50% of gain at 14.5–48%, coefficient applies | 100% of gain at IRC, no coefficient |
| AIMI | 0.7%+ only above €600,000 per owner | 0.4% from the first euro of VPT |
| Fixed overhead | Zero | €1,500–3,000 accounting yearly |

Two lines deserve the most attention. Interest deductibility is the company's genuine advantage for leveraged purchases, since personal category F ownership cannot deduct mortgage interest at all. And the extraction line is the company's genuine burden: profit is only spendable after the 28% dividend withholding, which stacks the effective rate on extracted rent above 42% before treaty relief.

Red flag: advice that compares headline rates, 20% IRC against 25% autonomous, and stops there. The extraction line converts the company's apparent 5-point advantage into a real deficit for owners who draw the rent, and it is the single commonest way foreign buyers talk themselves into the wrong structure.

## How does the rental year compare in real numbers?

Rental-year taxation typically favours the company only when leverage is high or costs are heavy, because the Lda's lower headline rate is consumed by extraction the moment profits leave the structure. Two worked comparisons on €24,000 of annual rent, one cash and one with a €280,000 loan, make the mechanics visible for this market.

Worked case study, unleveraged: a €400,000 Lisbon T2 renting at €2,000 monthly with €4,000 of deductible running costs. Personally, the taxable base is €20,000 and the tax at 25% is €5,000, leaving €15,000 in hand. Through an Lda, the base after the same costs and, say, €2,000 of accounting is €18,000, IRC at 16% takes €2,880, and extracting the €15,120 balance as dividends costs €4,234 more, leaving €10,886. The company owner paid €7,114 against the individual's €5,000 and worked harder for the privilege.

Worked case study, leveraged: the same apartment bought with a €280,000 loan at 3.8% pays roughly €10,600 of first-year interest. Personally that interest is invisible to the tax computation, so the bill stays €5,000. Inside the company it deducts, cutting the base to about €7,400 and IRC to €1,184; with profits retained rather than extracted, the company's annual tax is €3,816 lower. Retention is the hinge: the leveraged company wins while profits stay in and compound into the next purchase, and loses the advantage the year the shareholder wants the cash out. The [rental tax rules](/guides/non-resident-rental-income-tax-portugal/) cover the personal side line by line.

| Scenario | Personal tax, year 1 | Lda tax, year 1 | Winner |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash purchase, €24,000 rent | €5,000 | €7,114 with profits extracted | Personal, by €2,114 |
| €280,000 loan at 3.8%, profits retained | €5,000 | €1,184 | Lda, by €3,816 a year |

## What does AIMI do to each structure every year?

AIMI treats the two structures oppositely: individuals enjoy a €600,000 allowance per owner and pay 0.7% to 1.5% only above it, while companies pay a flat 0.4% on residential holdings from the first euro of taxable value, every year with no allowance. The area where each wins is readable straight off the portfolio's VPT.

The crossover arithmetic:

- **A couple owning a €900,000 VPT portfolio personally** holds two €600,000 allowances, pays AIMI on nothing, and the annual charge is zero.
- **The same portfolio in an Lda** pays 0.4% on the full €900,000: €3,600 a year, €36,000 over a decade, before any other consideration.
- **A single owner at €1,400,000 of VPT** pays individually on €800,000 at graduated rates, roughly €6,600; the company pays €5,600. Only at this scale, held by one person, does the corporate AIMI line win.
- **Blacklist entities** pay 7.5% AIMI and 7.5% IMI annually plus 10% IMT at purchase, which ends the offshore conversation before it starts.

Insider tip: joint personal ownership is the quiet winner of this table, because a married couple doubles the allowance to €1,200,000 of VPT without creating any structure, paying any accountant or filing anything beyond the ordinary. The full band mechanics are in the [AIMI guide](/guides/aimi-wealth-tax-portugal-property/), and they compound with IMI, which both structures pay identically at 0.3% to 0.45% of VPT.

## What happens at exit under each structure?

Exit taxation is where personal ownership banks its largest advantage in this market: an individual is taxed on 50% of the net gain at progressive rates with an inflation coefficient uplifting the cost base after 24 months, while a company pays IRC on 100% of the gain with no coefficient and then faces the extraction layer on distributing the proceeds.

Worked case study: a property bought at €300,000 and sold at €500,000 after 8 years, with €30,000 of documented costs and works. Personally, the indexed base and deductions produce a net gain near €150,000, half of which, €75,000, is taxed at the seller's bands for roughly €25,000 in a typical file. In an Lda, the book gain after costs is €170,000, IRC at 20% takes €34,000, and paying the remaining €136,000 out as dividends costs €38,080 more: €72,080 of total tax against €25,000, nearly three times as much, unless the proceeds are reinvested inside the company. Corporate reinvestment relief can halve the taxed portion when the full proceeds roll into qualifying assets, which is exactly why the structure suits perpetual reinvestors and punishes one-off sellers.

| Exit line | Personal | Lda |
|---|---|---|
| Taxed portion of gain | 50% | 100% |
| Inflation coefficient | Yes, after 24 months | No |
| Tax on €170,000 book gain | ≈ €25,000 | ≈ €72,000 extracted |
| Reinvestment relief | HPP and 2026–2029 rental routes | 50% exclusion on full corporate reinvestment |

Selling the shares instead of the property is the classic workaround and the classic disappointment: the buyer pool for Portuguese property-company shares is a fraction of the buyer pool for apartments, buyers discount for latent tax inside the company, and lenders finance shares reluctantly. Model the exit through the [mais-valias framework](/guides/mais-valias-portugal-capital-gains/) before letting the rental-year comparison decide.

## When does the company genuinely win?

Company structures earn their overhead in four situations, and portfolio scale is the common thread: from roughly 3 properties the fixed €1,500 to €3,000 of certified accounting spreads thin enough, and operational deductions start outweighing the 28% extraction cost for owners in this market who reinvest rather than draw the profits out.

The checklist for when an Lda deserves serious modelling:

- **Three or more properties**, or a clear plan to reach that scale, with profits recycled into acquisitions rather than extracted.
- **Meaningful leverage**, since interest deductibility is the one advantage personal ownership can never replicate.
- **A genuine business**, licensed Alojamento Local at scale, renovation-and-resale, or development, where activity taxation fits corporate form anyway.
- **Succession planning across borders**, where transferring shares in steps suits the family better than transferring bricks, taken with specialist advice against the [inheritance rules](/guides/portugal-property-inheritance-tax-foreigners/).

Red flag: advisers who lead with the structure before the numbers, and especially any proposal involving a jurisdiction on Portugal's blacklist. The 10% IMT, 7.5% IMI and 7.5% AIMI penal rates exist precisely for those schemes, and unwinding one later is a full taxable transfer. A second red flag is the single-apartment Lda sold as a privacy device: the shareholder registry, the central beneficial-ownership register and the bank's KYC all see through it, and the only party who reliably profits is the accountant.

## What about holding through a foreign company?

Foreign-company ownership is legal and mostly pointless for a simple rental in this market, because Portugal taxes the property where it stands: a Dutch BV holding a Lisbon apartment files Portuguese returns on the rent, pays Portuguese tax on the gain, and adds a second country's accounting at €2,000 or more a year on top of the obligations it hoped to simplify.

The comparison against a Portuguese Lda comes down to friction:

- **A permanent establishment question** follows any foreign company actively letting Portuguese property, and the answer usually creates Portuguese filing anyway.
- **Banks price the opacity.** Portuguese lenders finance foreign-company purchases reluctantly and at lower loan-to-value, where they finance them at all.
- **Notaries and registries process them slowly.** Apostilled corporate documents, translated resolutions and beneficial-ownership evidence add weeks to a completion timeline.
- **The blacklist rates sit one bad choice away.** Route the same idea through a listed jurisdiction and the purchase pays 10% IMT while the holding pays 7.5% IMI and 7.5% AIMI annually.

Worked case study: a German buyer holding through an existing GmbH to avoid creating a Portuguese entity saves the €360 incorporation and gains a second accounting bill in Munich, Portuguese non-resident filings for the GmbH, apostille costs on every corporate act, and a completion that took 5 weeks longer than a personal purchase. The all-in overhead exceeded a Portuguese Lda's within the first year, and the structure still paid Portuguese tax on every euro of rent. Where a foreign company already owns other assets and the Portuguese property genuinely belongs inside that group, the structure can be coherent; created for the purpose, it rarely is.

Portuguese Estate models these decisions on the file's actual numbers rather than on structure preferences, which is why the modelling questions above come before any incorporation step below.

## How do you set the structure up correctly at purchase?

Setup sequencing matters more than setup cost, because the structure must exist and hold funds before the CPCV is signed in its name: incorporation through Empresa na Hora costs €360 and a day, but banking, capital deposit and beneficial-owner registration realistically take 3 to 6 weeks for non-resident shareholders in this market.

The setup checklist, in the order that works:

1. **Model both structures on your actual numbers** for the rental years and the intended exit, not on headline rates.
2. **Incorporate the Lda**, minimum capital from €1 per quota in law and a few thousand euros in practice, with the certified accountant appointed from day one.
3. **Open the corporate bank account and clear KYC**, the slowest step for foreign shareholders, before any deadline depends on it.
4. **Register beneficial ownership** in the RCBE, which notaries check before deeds.
5. **Sign the CPCV in the company's name.** A contract signed personally cannot be flipped to the company later without a taxable assignment.
6. **Complete, then run the discipline**: separate accounts, documented management, invoices to the company's NIF, because a commingled structure loses its deductions in exactly the audit where they matter.

Personal buyers skip all six steps, which is itself part of the comparison. The purchase mechanics otherwise match the standard route in the [foreign buyer's guide](/guides/buy-property-portugal-foreigner/), with the same IMT, stamp duty and notarial sequence applying to a company buyer at the same rates, blacklist cases aside. Modelling both scenarios on the actual numbers before the CPCV costs an afternoon; unwinding an Lda after the deed costs a taxable transfer plus 2 sets of fees, and that asymmetry decides most close calls.
