  - question: "What are mais-valias in Portuguese property tax?"
    answer: "Mais-valias are capital gains, the taxable profit when you sell a property for more than its adjusted cost. For individuals, 50% of the net gain is added to income and taxed at progressive rates of 14.5–48%. The other 50% is permanently exempt."
  - question: "How is the mais-valias gain calculated exactly?"
    answer: "Gain = sale value − (acquisition value × inflation coefficient) − acquisition charges (IMT, stamp duty, notary) − documented improvement works from the last 12 years − selling costs (commission with VAT, energy certificate). Half of that result is then taxed at your marginal rate."
  - question: "What is the inflation coefficient (coeficiente de desvalorização da moeda)?"
    answer: "An official multiplier published each year by Portaria that indexes your purchase price for inflation. It applies whenever you held the property more than 24 months. A property bought a decade ago typically carries a coefficient around 1.25–1.35, cutting the taxable gain substantially."
  - question: "Which renovation costs can I deduct from the gain?"
    answer: "Improvement and maintenance works invoiced within the 12 years before the sale, plus the acquisition-era IMT, stamp duty and notary fees, plus selling costs. Every item needs a fatura showing your NIF and the property's article number, AT rejects undocumented amounts in full."
  - question: "Do non-residents still pay 28% flat on the full gain?"
    answer: "No. That regime ended on 1 January 2023 after CJEU case C-388/19 and Law 12/2022. Non-residents now include 50% of the gain at progressive rates like residents, but worldwide income is counted when setting the bracket."
  - question: "When do I file and pay mais-valias tax?"
    answer: "File Modelo 3 with Annex G between 1 April and 30 June of the year after the sale. AT issues the assessment by 31 July and payment is due by 31 August. Filing is mandatory even when the sale produced a loss."
  - question: "Is there a primary-residence exemption on mais-valias?"
    answer: "Yes. Gains on your own permanent home (HPP) escape tax if you reinvest the net proceeds into another HPP in Portugal, the EU or EEA, within 36 months after or 24 months before the sale. Partial reinvestment gives partial relief."
  - question: "What is the new 2026 rental reinvestment exemption?"
    answer: "DL 97/2026 exempts gains on housing sold from 1 January 2026 to 31 December 2029 if proceeds are reinvested in a Portuguese home let within 6 months at up to €2,300/month, kept let 36 months within 5 years, and not sold or gifted for 5 years."
  - question: "Can AT tax me on a higher value than my sale price?"
    answer: "Yes. If the VPT at the date of sale exceeds the declared price, Article 44(2) CIRS makes the VPT the sale value for the calculation, unless you prove the real price through the Article 139 procedure. Always check the caderneta before pricing."
  - question: "Are losses on Portuguese property sales usable?"
    answer: "Losses reported in Annex G can be carried forward against Portuguese property gains for the following 5 years, provided you filed the loss year's return on time. Non-residents keep this right, one more reason the mandatory filing works in your favour."

Mais-valias arithmetic decides more money than any negotiation in a Portuguese sale, yet most sellers meet the formula for the first time inside their Annex G assessment. The rules are mechanical: five subtractions, one halving, one progressive scale running from 14.5% to 48%. What moves the outcome by tens of thousands of euros is which invoices you kept, which inflation coefficient your purchase year carries, and whether the VPT quietly overtook your deed price during the 2024–2025 municipal revaluations. On the worked example below, complete paperwork produces roughly €5,300 of tax on a €150,000 gross gain, 3.5%, while the identical sale with missing faturas pays €11,900. The examples below follow the CIRS articles in the exact order the tax office applies them: value, indexation, charges, works, selling costs, halving, scale.

## What is the mais-valias formula, step by step?

Mais-valias tax in Portugal is charged on 50% of the net gain at progressive rates from 14.5% to 48%, and the net gain is built through five subtractions from the sale value. Foreign buyers who later sell use exactly the same formula as residents, the 28% flat regime ended in 2023 across this market.

| Step | Component | Legal basis |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Sale value, higher of price and VPT | Art. 44 CIRS |
| 2 | − Acquisition value × inflation coefficient | Art. 50 CIRS |
| 3 | − Acquisition charges: IMT, stamp duty, notary | Art. 51 CIRS |
| 4 | − Improvement works, last 12 years, invoiced | Art. 51 CIRS |
| 5 | − Selling costs: commission + VAT, energy certificate | Art. 51 CIRS |
| 6 | Result × 50%, taxed at 14.5–48% | Art. 43 CIRS |

Three details in that table decide most disputes, treat them as a pre-filing checklist, because each one decides cases on its own. The advantages of the current regime all sit in the deduction lines; the disadvantages sit in what happens without paperwork, the [full cost stack you paid when buying](/guides/cost-of-buying-property-portugal/) only shrinks the gain if the 2018 invoices still exist. The coefficient applies only when you held the property more than 24 months. Works older than 12 years vanish from the calculation regardless of value. And the sale value is the higher of the deed price and the VPT, a rule that catches sellers in recently revalued areas like Lisbon and Cascais.

Part-shares follow the same mechanics proportionally. Selling a 50% quota applies every line at half weight, and married couples filing jointly split the gain across both incomes, which frequently drops each half a bracket. An inherited quota and a purchased quota in the same property carry different acquisition dates and different coefficients, and Annex G takes them as separate rows, not an average.

The rate scale then runs 14.5%, 16.5%, 22%, 25%, 32%, 35.5%, 43.5%, 45% and 48%, with a solidarity surcharge of 2.5% above €80,000 and 5% above €250,000 of taxable income. For non-residents, worldwide income is counted to locate the bracket even though only the Portuguese gain is taxed, a German salary pushes a Portuguese gain into a higher band.

## How does a worked example look for a typical resale?

A worked example makes the formula concrete: an apartment bought for €300,000 in 2018 and sold for €450,000 in 2026 typically produces near €5,300 of tax, not the €30,000 sellers fear, indexation, charges, works and selling costs strip the gross gain first. The area's headline gain and the taxable gain are different animals.

Case study, Lisbon T2, held 8 years:

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Sale price (VPT lower) | €450,000 |
| Acquisition 2018 | €300,000 |
| × coefficient (assume 1.16 from the annual Portaria) | −€348,000 |
| Acquisition charges paid in 2018 (IMT €13,272 + stamp €2,400 + notary €1,000) | −€16,672 |
| Documented works 2019–2024 (kitchen, windows) | −€25,000 |
| Selling costs (4% + 23% VAT + CE €300) | −€22,440 |
| **Net gain** | **€37,888** |
| Taxable half | €18,944 |
| Tax at ~28% average marginal | **≈ €5,300** |

Effective burden: 3.5% of the gross €150,000 gain. Strip the €25,000 of works and €16,672 of charges because the invoices are missing, and the net gain jumps to €79,560, tax roughly €11,900. Identical apartment, identical sale, €6,600 more tax purely from paperwork. Missing faturas are the single most expensive omission a seller makes.

Insider tip: the coefficient table is published every autumn by Portaria and AT applies it automatically. Improvement works are indexed too, by the coefficient of the year each invoice was paid, not the purchase year, under Article 50(2) CIRS. Self-filed returns miss that distinction constantly: a €40,000 renovation paid in 2018 enters the 2026 formula uplifted by the 2018 coefficient, and applying the wrong year misstates the gain by thousands in either direction. Model both versions before accepting an agent's "your tax will be about X" estimate; agents systematically quote the frightening number.

## What changed for non-residents after 2023?

Non-resident sellers are taxed on the resident basis, 50% inclusion at progressive rates, since 1 January 2023, when Law 12/2022 executed the CJEU's C-388/19 ruling and AT confirmed the practice in Ofício Circulado 20255. Foreign buyers who sell today face neither the old 28% flat rate nor any surcharge specific to this market.

The change cuts both ways, and a checklist keeps the comparison honest:

- **Gains shrink for most.** Under the old regime a €100,000 gain paid €28,000 flat. Under the current one, 50% inclusion at even the top 48% band caps the burden at €24,000, and real cases land far lower.
- **Worldwide income sets the rate.** A high foreign salary can push the Portuguese half-gain into the 43.5–48% bands. Retirees with modest pensions often land near 25–32%.
- **Losses became usable.** Annex G losses carry forward 5 years against future Portuguese gains, file on time to preserve them.
- **Refund claims for 2019–2022 sales are closed.** The four-year revision window under Article 78 LGT has run out for the old flat-rate years; what was not reclaimed is gone.

Worked scenario: a UK couple selling a €380,000 Tavira townhouse in 2026 with a €60,000 net gain includes €30,000 in Portuguese income. With UK pensions of €40,000 counted for rate purposes, the blended marginal rate lands near 30%, about €9,000, versus €16,800 under the pre-2023 regime. The UK–Portugal treaty then credits the Portuguese tax against any UK CGT on the same disposal.

## Which exemptions can reduce the bill to zero?

Portuguese law offers three routes that reduce or eliminate mais-valias, and between 2026 and 2029 the newest one is open to landlords rather than owner-occupiers only. Eligibility is mechanical rather than discretionary, so foreign buyers in this market should test all three routes before filing, the checklist below ranks them by breadth.

- **Primary-residence reinvestment (Art. 10(5) CIRS).** Sell your permanent home (HPP), reinvest the net proceeds into another HPP in Portugal, the EU or EEA within 36 months after (or 24 before) the sale, and the reinvested share of the gain escapes tax. Partial reinvestment, partial relief. Requires the property to have been your registered HPP, address on your tax file, typically for at least 24 months.
- **DL 97/2026 rental reinvestment.** Gains on housing sold 1 January 2026 – 31 December 2029 are exempt when proceeds go into a Portuguese home let within 6 months at rent up to €2,300/month, kept let for 36 months within the first 5 years, with no sale or gift for 5 years. The full mechanics sit in the [selling guide](/guides/sell-property-portugal-non-resident/), and the rate framework in the [capital gains overview](/guides/portugal-capital-gains-tax-property/).
- **Pre-1989 acquisitions.** Property acquired before 1 January 1989, the year the CIRS entered force, sits outside mais-valias entirely; the sale is reported in Anexo G1 rather than Anexo G, and no tax falls due.

DL 97/2026 also excludes gains on building land and secondary homes sold for housing use during the same 2026–2029 window, a route relevant to plot owners in growth municipalities. Conditions are strict and the diploma is young; treat AT binding rulings (informações vinculativas, answered within 90 days) as cheap insurance, the fee is €25 to €250.

Red flag: the 65-or-retired reinvestment variant, placing HPP proceeds into a pension product or insurance contract within 6 months, is often pitched by advisers to sellers who do not meet the age or contract conditions. Verify against Art. 10(7) CIRS before relying on it.

## How do inherited and long-held properties change the numbers?

Inherited and long-held properties follow the same five-step formula but with different starting values, and the differences typically cut the tax rather than raise it, a pre-1989 purchase escapes mais-valias entirely, while an inherited property resets its acquisition value to the VPT used for stamp duty at the succession date. Foreign buyers who inherited Portuguese property routinely overestimate the bill.

| Scenario | Acquisition value used | Coefficient applies from |
|---|---|---|
| Bought before 1 Jan 1989 | Not taxed, declared in Anexo G1 | n/a |
| Inherited | VPT at date of death (stamp duty base) | Year of death |
| Gifted | VPT used for stamp duty, with anti-abuse look-back | Year of gift |
| Bought off-plan | Price in the escritura, not the CPCV | Year of the deed |

Worked scenario: a Brazilian heir selling a Cascais T3 for €600,000 in 2026, inherited in 2014 when the VPT stood at €310,000. Indexed at an assumed coefficient of 1.30, the acquisition line becomes €403,000; with €19,000 of documented works and €30,000 of selling costs, the net gain lands at €148,000, €74,000 taxable, roughly €29,000 of tax at the upper brackets. The same seller assuming the parents' original 1998 purchase price of €120,000 as the base would have modelled €195,000 of taxable gain and nearly €80,000 of tax: three times the real liability, and reason enough to check the succession VPT before pricing the sale.

Two long-hold rules reward patience in this market, and both interact with the [renovation invoices you keep](/guides/renovation-costs-portugal-per-m2/) and the [buyer-side taxes you once paid](/guides/imt-tax-non-resident-portugal-2026/). The inflation coefficient compounds with time, acquisitions a decade back typically carry 1.25–1.35, and 1990s purchases run past 1.8, and works invoices stay deductible for 12 years, so a renovation-then-sell strategy keeps its paperwork value across a full market cycle. Long-held sellers who document everything land effective rates of 3–7% of the gross gain; the 48% headline rate exists mostly in headlines. Heirs selling within 24 months of the succession lose the coefficient entirely, waiting past the two-year mark before completing is often worth four figures on its own, and costs nothing but patience.

## How and when do you file Annex G?

Annex G of Modelo 3 is the mandatory capital gains schedule, filed between 1 April and 30 June of the year after the sale, with assessment by 31 July and payment due by 31 August. Filing is required in this market even at a loss, skipping it costs €200 minimum, and non-EU sellers file through a fiscal representative.

The filing sequence:

1. Register the sale details: article number from the caderneta, sale and acquisition dates and values, quota sold.
2. List charges and works line by line: AT cross-checks against e-fatura, so invoices carrying your NIF and the property's article number sail through; the rest get queried.
3. Declare any reinvestment intention in the year of sale: the exemption dies if the intention box is never ticked, even when the reinvestment itself happens on time.
4. Non-residents tick the residence field and attach nothing: evidence goes in only if AT opens a divergência, which typically arrives within 8 months.
5. Pay by 31 August or set a payment plan of up to 36 instalments for amounts over €204.

Penalties escalate fast once the 30 June deadline passes: €200 to €2,500 for the late return itself, compensatory interest at 4% a year on unpaid tax, and, worst of all, an AT-drafted assessment built from notarial data with zero deductions applied, which routinely doubles or triples the correct liability. A seller with a genuine loss who skips filing converts a €0 bill into a four-figure one, and simultaneously forfeits the 5-year loss carry-forward that would have sheltered a future gain. The pattern repeats every filing season: the sale completes in autumn, the seller flies home, the April–June window opens and closes unnoticed, and the divergência letter lands 8 months later at a foreign address nobody checks. Diarise the deadline on the day of the escritura and the entire problem disappears for the cost of one calendar entry.
