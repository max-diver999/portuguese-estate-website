The structural fact that governs everything else on this page: in Portugal the agent works for the seller. Not out of bad faith, but by contract. The mediation agreement is signed with the owner, the fee comes out of the sale proceeds, and it is only earned if the deal closes.

That does not make agents adversaries. It makes them a party with a known interest, which is a normal and manageable thing in a transaction, provided you know it. The buyers who get hurt are the ones who treat the agent as their adviser.

## What Commission Costs, and Who Pays

| | Position in Portugal |
|---|---|
| Set by law | No, negotiated between seller and agency |
| Typical range | About 3% to 6% of sale price |
| Most common traditional figure | Around 5%, plus 23% IVA |
| Who pays | The seller, from the proceeds at the deed |
| When earned | On completion, not on agreement |
| Buyer-side fee | None in the standard model |

On a EUR 500,000 sale at 5% plus IVA the seller pays EUR 30,750. On a EUR 1,500,000 sale the same percentage is EUR 92,250, which is why high-value mandates are negotiated down and why the effective rate falls as the price rises.

The buyer pays nothing directly and pays it all indirectly. The commission is a cost of sale the seller carries and prices into their expectations, so a low-commission arrangement occasionally shows up as a slightly softer asking price and usually does not. Treat it as background, not as a lever you control.

Your own costs are a separate list: 7.5% IMT for non-residents completing on or after 1 September 2026, 0.8% stamp duty, notary and registry, and 1% to 1.5% in legal fees. The full breakdown is in [the cost of buying property in Portugal](/guides/cost-of-buying-property-portugal/) and the tax position in [IMT for non-residents](/guides/imt-tax-non-resident-portugal-2026/).

## The AMI Licence

Every real estate mediation business operating in Portugal must hold an AMI licence issued by IMPIC, the Instituto dos Mercados Públicos, do Imobiliário e da Construção. The licence carries a number, appears on the agency's listings and contracts, and comes with a compulsory civil liability insurance requirement.

| Check | How | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| AMI number exists | IMPIC's public register | Confirms the business is licensed at all |
| Name matches | Register entry against your paperwork | Catches a licence borrowed from an associated company |
| Insurance current | Ask, and ask for evidence | The cover is compulsory and it lapses |
| Status active | Register entry | Licences are suspended and cancelled |
| Mediation contract held | Ask directly | Tells you whose property this actually is |

The last row is the one that changes behaviour. An agency showing you a property may hold the mandate, may be sharing another agency's mandate, or may simply have seen the listing and hoped to place you. All three happen. Only the first gives you a direct line to the seller, and it is a fair and normal question to ask before you spend a day viewing.

Verifying the number takes about two minutes and international buyers almost never do it. It is the cheapest piece of due diligence in the entire purchase.

## Listing Agent, Buyer's Agent, or Neither

| | Listing agent | Buyer's agent | Lawyer only |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whose contract | The seller's | Yours | Yours |
| Who pays | Seller | You, typically a fee or a share | You, 1% to 1.5% |
| Access to stock | Their own mandates | Broad, including off-portal | None, not their job |
| Negotiates for you | No | Yes | No |
| Checks the title | No | No | Yes |
| Best where | Portal-visible stock, you can visit | Opaque or off-market segments | Everywhere, always |

The buyer's agent model is well established in the Algarve and the Lisbon prime segment and much thinner elsewhere. It earns its fee in three situations: you cannot view in person, the segment has significant off-portal stock, or you are buying at a level where a two-percent negotiation swing dwarfs the fee.

It earns much less where the stock is fully listed on the portals and you can fly in. In that case the money is better spent on a good independent lawyer, who is the only one of the three who actually verifies what you are buying.

Note the row that is blank in two of three columns. No agent, of either kind, checks the title.

## What the Agent Does Not Do

A Portuguese estate agent introduces a property and coordinates the sale, and does none of the verification a buyer assumes is happening. Legal due diligence, survey, valuation and tax advice are four separate professionals, and the agent is not any of them. This is where the expensive misunderstandings live.

- **Legal due diligence.** Caderneta predial against the Conservatória registry, charges, licensing, habitation certificate. That is your lawyer's work, set out in [due diligence on Portuguese property](/guides/due-diligence-portugal-property/).
- **Survey.** Structural condition, damp, roof, services. A separate engineer or surveyor, and rarely commissioned by international buyers who should.
- **Valuation.** An asking price is not a valuation. A bank valuation serves the bank, not you.
- **Tax.** Your IMT band, your residency position, your AIMI exposure, your letting tax. None of it is the agent's function.
- **The CPCV.** The promissory contract is where your deposit becomes committed, covered in [the CPCV guide](/guides/cpcv-promissory-contract-portugal/). Never sign one your own lawyer has not read.

A cheerful, competent, well-meaning agent can do none of these things, and it is not a criticism to say so. Buyers get into trouble by assuming the fifth item on that list is covered because the first four went smoothly.

## Exclusive and Non-Exclusive Mandates

The mandate type is invisible to buyers and explains most of what confuses them about the Portuguese market. An exclusive mandate gives one agency the property, usually at a negotiated rate below the standard 5%. A non-exclusive mandate lets any agency market it, which is why one villa appears at four prices.

| | Exclusive mandate | Non-exclusive mandate |
|---|---|---|
| Agencies marketing it | One | Any number |
| Price consistency | One asking price | Frequently several |
| Commission | Usually negotiated lower | Usually at the standard rate |
| Agent effort | Higher, the fee is secured | Lower, the fee may go elsewhere |
| Who you deal with | A single point | Whoever answered first |

Under a non-exclusive mandate, the agency that introduces the buyer earns the fee, which is why the same villa appears on six sites at four prices and why an agent who has never met the owner will still offer to show it to you. It also explains the urgency: an agent working a shared mandate is racing other agencies, not you.

Under an exclusive mandate the agency has a real relationship with the seller, knows the history, and can tell you what an offer will actually do. That is worth finding, and asking who holds the mandate is how you find it.

## What the Agent Does Between Offer and Deed

The Portuguese purchase runs a defined sequence from viewing to escritura, and the agent's role in it is smaller than international buyers expect. Their usefulness peaks before the offer is accepted and falls away sharply afterwards, which is the opposite of where most buyers place their trust.

| Stage | Agent's role | Who actually does the work |
|---|---|---|
| Viewing and offer | Central | The agent |
| Offer accepted | Relays and coordinates | The agent |
| Due diligence | None | Your lawyer |
| CPCV drafting | Sometimes supplies a template | Your lawyer, always |
| Deposit paid | Coordinates timing | Your lawyer and the bank |
| Mortgage | Introductions at most | Bank and broker |
| Escritura at the notary | Attends, often | Notary and your lawyer |
| After completion | None | You, your lawyer, your accountant |

The template row is the trap. A CPCV template supplied by the agency is drafted for a transaction to complete, not for your protection in it, and the deposit clause is where that difference shows. The sequence and the clauses that matter are in [the escritura and notary guide](/guides/escritura-notary-portugal-property/).

Two practical notes. Portuguese estate agents are generally responsive and hands-on compared with several other European markets, and that is a genuine strength when you are coordinating remotely across a time zone. And their usefulness peaks before the offer is accepted, then declines sharply, which is the opposite of where most buyers place their trust.

## Selling Later, From the Other Side

Every buyer eventually becomes a seller, and the commission that was invisible on the way in becomes a real line on the way out. At around 5% plus 23% IVA, an exit on a EUR 500,000 property costs EUR 30,750 in agency fees alone, before capital gains and before any legal cost.

At around 5% plus 23% IVA, an exit on a EUR 500,000 property costs EUR 30,750 in agency fees alone, before capital gains, before the energy certificate, before any legal cost. Modelling a ten-year hold without that figure overstates the return by roughly six per cent of the sale price.

Three things reduce it. An exclusive mandate is normally negotiated below the standard rate, since the agency is not racing anyone. Higher values carry lower percentages, so the effective rate on a EUR 1,500,000 sale is usually well below 5%. And a property with clean, complete documentation sells faster, which strengthens your position when the rate is agreed.

That last point connects back to the purchase. The documentary work your lawyer does when you buy, licensing, the habitation certificate, the registry position, is the same work a buyer's lawyer will do to you a decade later. A file that was clean going in is worth real money coming out.

## The Portal Problem

Portuguese listings reach international buyers through idealista, Kyero, Rightmove Overseas and A Place in the Sun, and each layer adds distance from the source. Non-exclusive mandates mean one property can enter a portal average several times at its highest asking figure, which distorts any price read taken from it.

Three effects to plan around. The same property appears under several agencies at different asking prices, because non-exclusive mandates are common. Sold stock stays visible for months, which distorts any sense of what is actually available. And prices shown on international portals sometimes sit above the same property's Portuguese-language listing.

The practical habit is simple: before treating any portal figure as evidence, find the same property on a Portuguese-language listing and compare. For absolute market levels use the INE series with the quarter attached, as set out in [Portugal house prices](/guides/portugal-house-prices/), rather than a portal average that blends incomparable stock.

## Regional Differences

| Region | Agency landscape | What to expect |
|---|---|---|
| Algarve | Dense, heavily international, English throughout | Multiple agencies on one property, portal-first |
| Lisbon and Cascais | Mixed international and Portuguese | Prime segment has real off-portal stock |
| Porto and the north | More Portuguese-facing | Less English, better local knowledge |
| Silver Coast | Many small independents | Wide variation in professionalism |
| Interior and rural | Thin, often a single local agency | Local relationships matter more than portals |

The pattern is that the more international a market is, the more agencies compete over the same visible stock, and the less any one of them adds. The less international it is, the more a local agent knows that no portal shows, and the more the language gap costs you. ## Questions to Ask Before You Commit a Day

- What is your AMI number, and which company does it belong to?
- Do you hold the mediation contract on this property, or is it shared?
- How long has it been on the market, and has the asking price moved?
- Is the price on the Portuguese listing the same as the one I was shown?
- Who is the seller, and are they resident in Portugal?
- What is the licensing position, and is there a habitation certificate?
- Which of these answers will you put in writing?

The final question is the useful one. Anything an agent will not repeat in an email is worth treating as a hope rather than a fact.

## The Kinds of Agency You Will Meet

| Type | Who they are | Where they help |
|---|---|---|
| International franchise | RE/MAX, Century 21, Keller Williams and similar | Volume, process, English throughout |
| Portuguese national chain | Domestic networks with regional depth | Local stock, Portuguese-language listings |
| Expat-facing specialist | Built around British, Irish or Dutch buyers | Familiar process, narrower stock |
| Independent local | One or two offices, long-established | Off-portal knowledge, weakest online presence |
| Buyer's agent | Contracted to you, not the seller | Remote purchase, prime and off-market |

None of these categories is better than the others, and the licence requirement applies identically to all of them. What varies is the trade-off between stock breadth and local depth: a franchise office will show you everything on the portals, an independent in a small town will know the house that has not reached one yet.

The expat-facing specialist deserves one caution. Familiarity is genuinely valuable when you are buying in a second language, and it comes with a narrower selection filtered for what that agency believes foreign buyers want. The property you would have chosen may never be shown to you, which is an argument for looking at the Portuguese-language listings yourself even if you need a translation tool to do it.

## Risks and Red Flags

- **Pressure to sign a CPCV at the viewing.** No legitimate transaction requires it, and the deposit is the point of no easy return.
- **The agent recommending their own lawyer.** The conflict is structural. Instruct your own.
- **No AMI number on the paperwork.** Not a technicality. Stop.
- **A price only available "today".** Portuguese residential transactions do not work this way.
- **Reluctance to say who holds the mandate.** Usually means they do not.
- **Verbal assurance about licensing or legality.** Only the documents count, and only your lawyer reads them.

## Who Each Approach Suits

Three arrangements exist and the right one turns on two questions: whether you can view in person, and whether the stock you want appears on the portals. Where both answers are yes, a listing agent plus your own lawyer at 1% to 1.5% of price is sufficient. Where both answers are yes, a listing agent plus your own lawyer is enough. Where either is no, a buyer's agent earns their fee.

**Buyers who can visit and are shopping portal-visible stock.** A listing agent plus your own lawyer is sufficient, and the second is the one that matters.

**Buyers purchasing remotely, or in the prime segment.** A buyer's agent has a genuine job here: access, viewing on your behalf, and negotiation where the swing exceeds the fee.

**Buyers in the interior or rural markets.** A single good local agency beats any portal, and the constraint is language rather than fee.

**Anyone at any level.** An independent lawyer, always, from before the CPCV. The full sequence is in [how to buy property in Portugal step by step](/guides/how-to-buy-property-portugal-step-by-step/).
