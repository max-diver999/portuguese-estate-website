Treaty questions decide more money for British owners of Portuguese property than any negotiation over price, and the answers are older than most buyers expect: the convention dates from 1968, follows the OECD pattern, and survived Brexit untouched because it was never EU law. The structure is simple once stated. Portugal taxes what happens on Portuguese soil, the UK taxes its residents on worldwide income, and the credit mechanism stops the overlap from doubling the bill. Where owners lose money is in the mechanics: rent declared in one country but not the other, a gain reported to HMRC with no Portuguese assessment to credit, or a retirement move timed so that both countries claim a full year. The sections below follow the money in the order the two tax offices take it.

## Who taxes the rent from a Portuguese property?

Rental income from Portuguese property is taxed first in Portugal, because the treaty allocates income from immovable property to the country where it stands. In this market a UK-resident landlord pays 25% on long-let rent after limited deductions, then declares the same rent to HMRC and credits the Portuguese tax against the UK bill.

| Step | Where | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Portuguese filing | AT, annual Modelo 3 | 25% autonomous rate on long lets, limited expense deductions |
| 2. UK filing | Self Assessment, foreign pages | Rent recomputed under UK rules, UK expenses allowed |
| 3. Credit | UK side | Portuguese tax paid offsets the UK liability on that income |
| 4. Top-up | UK side | Payable only where the UK computation exceeds the Portuguese one |

The two computations rarely match, and the differences are structural. Portugal allows condominium charges, IMI, insurance and documented maintenance against the rent, but not mortgage interest. The UK allows a 20% basic-rate credit for finance costs and its own expense rules. A higher-rate UK taxpayer therefore usually tops up in the UK; a basic-rate taxpayer often finds the Portuguese 25% covers most of the UK bill.

Red flag: declaring the rent only in the UK is the most common cross-border error, and it fails in both directions. Portugal still assesses the tax with interest when the lease registration or utility data surfaces, and HMRC's credit claim collapses because there is no Portuguese assessment to credit. The order is fixed: Portugal first, credit second. Full Portuguese mechanics are in the [non-resident rental income guide](/guides/non-resident-rental-income-tax-portugal/).

## How is a sale taxed on both sides?

Capital gains on Portuguese property belong first to Portugal under the treaty, and for foreign buyers turned sellers the Portuguese calculation runs exactly as for anyone else: 50% of the net gain at progressive rates of 14.5% to 48%, with the inflation coefficient, acquisition charges, documented works and selling costs all deducted before the halving.

The two systems differ on four axes, and each one moves money:

- **Rate structure.** Portugal: 50% of the gain at 14.5% to 48%. UK: 18% or 24% flat on residential gains.
- **Currency.** Portugal computes in euros; the UK converts acquisition and disposal at their own dates' rates.
- **Reliefs.** Portugal indexes the cost base after 24 months of ownership; the UK applies the annual exempt amount instead.
- **Timing.** Portugal assesses the calendar year; the UK's 60-day property return runs from completion for UK property but Portuguese sales go through Self Assessment.

The UK taxes the disposal on the sterling gain, currently 18% or 24% on residential property depending on the seller's band. Two conversions matter here and neither is optional: HMRC computes the gain by converting the acquisition price at the historic exchange rate and the sale price at the disposal-date rate, so a currency movement can create a UK gain where the euro numbers show almost none, and the reverse.

Worked case study: a UK-resident couple sells a Tavira house for €380,000 with a Portuguese net gain of €60,000. Portugal assesses roughly €9,000 across the two half-shares at their bands. The UK computation, after the sterling conversion and both annual exempt amounts, produces say €13,500 of CGT at 24%. The treaty credit reduces that by the €9,000 paid in Portugal, leaving €4,500 to pay in the UK. Total burden: the higher system's number, paid in two parts. Skipping the Portuguese filing would not have saved the €9,000; it would have added penalties in Portugal and stripped the credit in the UK, raising the total to €22,500 plus fines. The full Portuguese formula sits in the [mais-valias worked examples](/guides/mais-valias-portugal-capital-gains/), and the filing route in the [Modelo 3 guide](/guides/modelo-3-anexo-g-property-sale/).

## What happens when both countries claim you as resident?

Dual residence is common in the year of a move, because the UK statutory residence test and Portugal's rule of 183 days or a habitual home can both be satisfied at once in this market, and the treaty then decides through a fixed sequence of four tie-breakers rather than through negotiation.

The sequence, applied in order until one test settles it:

- **Permanent home.** Where you have a home available in only one country, that country wins. A home kept in both moves the question down the list.
- **Centre of vital interests.** Family, employment, business ties and economic life. The strongest single factor in most real cases.
- **Habitual abode.** Where you actually spend your days across the period, counted from records rather than intentions. Portugal counts any day with an overnight stay; 183 days in any 12 months settles its domestic side.
- **Nationality.** Rarely reached; a British citizen who ties on everything above is treaty resident in the UK.

Treaty residence decides which country taxes worldwide income and which is limited to source taxation, but it removes no filing duties: a person treaty-resident in Portugal with UK rental income still files in both. The Portuguese side of the residence question, including the overnight-stay day count and the habitual-home limb that catches people below 183 days, is covered in the [183-day test guide](/guides/portugal-tax-residency-183-days/).

Insider tip: the tie-breaker runs on evidence, and the cheapest evidence is contemporaneous. Keep boarding passes, lease dates and utility activation records from the moving year itself. Reconstructing a centre-of-vital-interests argument two years later, against an assessment already issued, costs more in fees than the records would have cost in minutes.

## How are pensions and other income allocated?

Pension allocation is where the treaty surprises retirees most, because the 1968 text splits pensions by type rather than treating them as one category, and the split moves the whole bill between systems taxing at 14.5% to 48% on one side and 20% to 45% on the other. Foreign buyers planning retirement need the type-by-type table, not a single rate.

| Income type | Primary taxing right | Practical 2026 position |
|---|---|---|
| Private and occupational pensions | Portugal, once treaty resident there | Progressive 14.5% to 48%; NHR's 10% closed to new entrants |
| UK government-service pensions | UK only | Taxed by HMRC regardless of Portuguese residence |
| UK state pension | Portugal for treaty residents | Progressive rates, aggregated with other income |
| Dividends | Shared; source state capped | Treaty caps UK or Portuguese withholding at 10% to 15% |
| Interest | Shared; source capped at 10% | Credit method resolves the remainder |
| Employment income | Where the work is performed | Short UK workdays can stay UK-taxable |

Red flag: the government-service carve-out catches former civil servants, NHS staff, teachers and military retirees: those pensions stay UK-taxed even after a full move to Portugal, while a private pension from the same career length flips to Portuguese rates. A retirement plan that models everything at one country's rates is wrong for most mixed careers, and the error compounds annually: a €30,000 occupational pension modelled at UK basic rate but actually taxable in Portugal lands two brackets higher once aggregated with other income.

For buyers still choosing a route into Portugal, the residence decision interacts with the [D7 visa income requirements](/guides/portugal-d7-visa-property/) and with the property purchase itself, since becoming Portuguese tax resident within 24 months triggers the [IMT refund](/guides/imt-refund-tax-resident-portugal/) on the 7.5% non-resident rate paid at completion.

## What paperwork makes the credit actually work?

Credit relief is administrative, not automatic: HMRC grants it against evidence of Portuguese tax assessed and paid, and the Portuguese side of the file has to exist first. Assessments arrive within 4 months of a Modelo 3 filing, and AT can revisit them for 4 years, so the checklist below keeps the mechanism working for the whole exposure window:

- **Portuguese assessment notes** for each year, downloaded from the Portal das Finanças, showing the tax charged on the rent at 25% or on the halved gain.
- **Proof of payment**, since the credit follows tax paid, not tax merely assessed.
- **Certificates of residence** from the country claiming treaty residence, requested from HMRC online or from AT through the portal, dated for the relevant year.
- **Consistent figures across both returns.** The rent and gain declared to AT must reconcile with the foreign pages filed to HMRC; a mismatch above a few hundred euros is what triggers enquiries on both sides.
- **The Portuguese filing itself**, on time: Modelo 3 between 1 April and 30 June for the prior year's rent and gains, because a late Portuguese filing delays the assessment the UK credit depends on.

Timing is the quiet difficulty of the whole mechanism. The UK tax year ends on 5 April while Portugal assesses the calendar year, so one Portuguese assessment routinely straddles two UK returns, and the rent earned between January and April appears in different periods on each side of the claim. Where the Portuguese tax is not yet assessed when the UK return is due, HMRC accepts an estimate with a later amendment, but the amendment has to happen; an estimated credit never trued up is the audit finding that unwinds 3 or 4 years at once.

## What changes in the year you actually move?

Moving year taxation is the hardest single year in the whole cross-border relationship, because both countries apply split-year mechanics with different boundaries and the treaty tie-breaker sits on top of both. Foreign buyers who sequence the move deliberately typically save four to five figures against those who let the calendar decide.

Portugal applies residency from the day the conditions are met when the change of address is registered with that date, so income earned before arrival stays outside the Portuguese net. The UK's statutory residence test runs its own split-year cases on the other side. The gap between the two boundaries is where a final UK bonus, a share vesting or a property completion should be placed, deliberately rather than accidentally.

| Decision in the moving year | Done well | Done by default |
|---|---|---|
| Residency start date registered with AT | The actual arrival date | 1 January, sweeping in pre-move income |
| Final UK income events | Paid before Portuguese residency starts | Paid after, taxed at up to 48% |
| Portuguese property completion | Sequenced against the 24-month IMT refund clock | Unconnected to the residency plan |
| IFICI registration | By 15 January of the following year | Missed, losing a year of the 20% rate |

Sequencing rewards a short written plan more than any instrument, and the plan costs an hour against savings that routinely reach €10,000 to €40,000 on a single well-placed income event. A one-page timeline listing the UK exit date, the Portuguese registration date, each income event between them and the two filing deadlines catches almost every moving-year error before it happens, because the errors are all ordering errors. The UK's replacement of the remittance basis with the 4-year foreign income and gains regime from April 2025 changed the arithmetic for returners in the other direction too, so a plan drafted before 2025 deserves a fresh read rather than an assumption that it still holds. Cross-check the Portuguese half against the [183-day residency test](/guides/portugal-tax-residency-183-days/) and, where relocation is employment-driven, the [IFICI regime's deadlines](/guides/ifici-portugal-nhr-2-tax-regime/).

Insider tip: request the HMRC residence certificate and the AT one in the same week you file the first split-year return. Both offices date certificates to the period requested, and matching dates across the pair is what makes the first credit claim pass without correspondence.

Buyer scenario: a Manchester owner moving in May sells UK investments in April while still UK-resident, lets the Algarve house from July, and claims split-year treatment on both sides. Portugal taxes the rent first from day 1 of residency, and the UK's claim on that rent ends with the move date.
